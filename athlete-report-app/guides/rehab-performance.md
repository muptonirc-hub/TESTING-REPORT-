# Rehab and performance in one program: priorities, session order and the blend over time (BASE Health Noosa guide library)

**Status: draft 1, 3 October 2026, written from published evidence for the clinic to review and edit.** Sources at the end with DOI/PMID (every source checked against its Europe PMC record); tags like [T13] point to them. Items marked *(consensus)* are expert agreement, *(practice)* are common clinical practice without a trial behind them, and *(inference)* are this guide's own reasoning from the evidence.

**Scope and use.** What to do when one report shows both a condition (a diagnosis the physiotherapist has given the program, with its stage) and performance findings (power, strength, reactive strength, speed, symmetry) that may or may not belong to it. This guide tells Claude how to read the report as a whole: which findings the condition explains, what leads in the current block, what runs alongside it, how the sections are ordered in a session, and how the emphasis moves from rehab-led to performance-led as the stage changes. It does not replace the condition guides (they hold the stage tables, pain rules and criteria), the rehabilitation-principles guide (loading, pain, symmetry, return-to-sport decisions) or the training-variables guide (dosing for each adaptation); it tells Claude how to combine them. The physiotherapist owns the diagnosis, the stage, the side and every return-to-sport decision; Claude drafts a program within them and explains its reasoning so the physiotherapist can change it.

---

## 1. Rules that matter most

1. **Read the whole report before choosing anything, and sort the findings into three groups**: findings the condition plausibly explains (same side or region, a quality the condition is known to lower at this stage: section 3); deficits independent of the condition (other side, other region, or a quality the condition does not touch); and clear strengths worth keeping. The program is built from the groups, not from the findings one by one *(inference from T10, T11, T13, T18–T25)*.
2. **A deficit the condition explains is treated through the condition's own progression, not chased with a separate performance exercise.** After ACL reconstruction the operated leg's quadriceps are weaker (effect size −0.78 against the other leg) with activation deficits in both legs for years [T18]; Achilles tendinopathy lowers plantar-flexor strength by 18–44%, hopping reactive strength by 16–35% and rate of force development by 10–21% [T20]; patellar tendinopathy lowers isometric and concentric knee-extensor strength (g 0.54 and 0.78) [T21]; eccentric hamstring deficits persist after return from a hamstring strain [T19]. A low jump, hop or Nordic number on that side is the injury talking; the rehab section already contains its treatment, and the stage's rows decide the dose *(inference)*.
3. **An independent deficit gets its own work now, as long as it does not load the affected tissue beyond the stage's rules.** The injured athlete's fitness is preserved from the first days: physical-fitness preservation is one of the six early-stage dimensions after ACL reconstruction and reconditioning one of the three mid-stage areas [T10, T11]; strength and conditioning principles belong in every phase of rehabilitation [T2]. Keep the rest of the athlete training *(consensus)*.
4. **Treating the athlete's own deficits and risk factors from the start, by criteria, beats a general protocol.** In the only trial of its kind, 48 footballers with an acute hamstring strain followed either an individualised, multifactorial, criteria-based algorithm (lumbo-pelvic control, strength and sprint mechanics addressed together, performance-oriented from the early stage) or a validated general protocol built around long-length strength work: reinjuries within six months of return were 1 versus 6 (relative risk 6), return to sport was slightly slower (25.5 versus 23.2 days) and sprint speed and horizontal power at return were better [T13]. The lesson for a report: the findings decide the content; a slightly longer, better-aimed program is the safer one.
5. **The condition's stage sets the ceiling for everything in the session**, including the performance section: nothing from a later stage row, the stage's pain rules, no energy-storage or sprint work for a tendon or muscle that is not yet at that stage (condition guides; rehab-principles.md rules 1–4). Red lines in a guide stop the program, not just that section.
6. **Priority for the block is stated, not implied.** Early and middle stages: the condition leads; the performance section is small, keeps the other limb and the untouched qualities, and avoids the injured tissue's high-strain loads. Late stage: the performance section grows and the condition's work becomes its progression (energy storage, sprinting, change of direction) so the two sections merge. Ongoing: performance leads with a maintenance dose for the condition. This mirrors the return-to-sport continuum (return to participation, to sport, to performance), which runs alongside rehabilitation rather than after it [T6, T14] *(consensus)*, the ACL frameworks' staged emphasis with entry criteria for each stage [T10, T11], and the case for periodising rehabilitation like training: phases with their own aims, and the variables programmed within each phase [T1, T3, T15] *(reasoned from training science; the scoping review found periodised rehab poorly reported, T16)*.
7. **Power rests on strength, and the least-developed quality gives the most.** An athlete cannot be powerful without being reasonably strong; focusing training on the weakest contributing factor produces the largest gain [T41]; stronger athletes (squat about 2.0 × body mass) gained jump-squat velocity faster than weaker ones (1.2 × body mass) in the first five weeks of combined weightlifting, plyometric and ballistic training [T40]. In a performance section for a weak athlete, strength leads and ballistic work stays light until a base exists; for a strong athlete with a low DSI or RSI, ballistic and reactive work leads (performance-tests.md).
8. **Session order: priority first, fast before heavy, heavy before long.** Strength before endurance in the same session gains about 7% more lower-body dynamic strength and 4 kg more 1RM than the reverse, with no cost to aerobic gains [T28, T29]; the exercise done first gains most (training-variables.md). The condition's loading sits where the clinic guide's session order puts it (eccentric and isometric work after the strength lifts, earlier when it is the main priority): isometrics for tendon pain can open the session or precede sport when pain relief is the aim, heavy slow tendon loading and hamstring eccentrics follow the power and heavy work, and Nordic work goes before or after training with the same strength gain (placement changes muscle architecture, not strength) [T30].
9. **Keep-up work needs intensity, not volume.** Strength and size are kept for up to 32 weeks with one session a week and one hard set per exercise if the load stays heavy [T27]; one or two heavy exercises for the uninjured qualities are enough. Training the other limb hard (cross-education) is an accepted adjunct for a one-sided injury; 13–18 sessions or 4–6 weeks of high-intensity or eccentric work gives a meaningful transfer [T26] *(consensus; pooled clinical effect small, rehab-principles.md)*.
10. **Load the athlete up, don't rest them down.** Athletes used to high chronic loads are injured less; under-training raises risk; rapid, excessive increases cause many non-contact injuries [T33, T34]; the IOC consensus treats poor load management as a major risk factor and asks for gradual, monitored progression [T35]. Do not steer by an acute:chronic ratio: it has no causal evidence and poor statistical properties [T32]; the running data for the "10% rule" are very limited (a >30% weekly increase versus <10% gave a hazard ratio of 1.6 with a confidence interval crossing 1) [T31]. Progress one variable at a time and judge by the response to the last session.
11. **Criteria open the next block; time is a floor.** After ACL reconstruction each month of delay to nine months cut reinjury by 51%, failing the return-to-sport battery meant 38% reinjury against 6% for passing, and more symmetrical quadriceps strength lowered reinjury [T36]; not meeting six discharge criteria meant four times the graft-rupture rate, and each 10% lower hamstring:quadriceps ratio raised it further [T37]. Even at nine months, professional footballers kept jump asymmetries in concentric impulse and force and eccentric deceleration (d 0.42–1.35) [T38]; functional tests predict "return to performance" only weakly [T39]. So the performance section opens up by the condition guide's criteria, and the rationale names the test that moves it on.
12. **Write the reasoning down.** The physiotherapist needs to see which findings were treated as the condition, which as separate, what leads this block, what the next block adds and the sign that moves it on. Return to play is a shared decision between clinician, athlete and coach [T12] *(consensus)*, and a draft that explains itself can be changed.

## 2. The reading pass: sorting the findings

Before any exercise is chosen, Claude sorts every flagged result (Off target, Close, Behind, or a notable asymmetry) into one of three groups, using the condition, its side and its stage, and section 3's table of expected deficits.

| Group | Test | What it means for the program |
|---|---|---|
| **Explained by the condition** | Same side or region as the condition, and a quality the condition is known to lower at this stage (section 3); or a symmetry test whose weaker side is the injured side | Treated inside the Rehab section by the stage's rows; named in the rationale as expected; not duplicated in the Performance section; re-tested as a progression criterion |
| **Independent deficit** | Other side; other region; or a quality the condition does not lower (for example low absolute IMTP force in both legs with a one-sided Achilles tendinopathy) | Gets its own Performance section now, chosen from the training-variables guide and the clinic guide, as long as it does not load the affected tissue beyond the stage's rules |
| **Strength to keep** | On target or strong; the qualities the sport needs most | At most one or two heavy exercises in a Keep-up section (or folded into Performance); a maintenance dose [T27] |

Rules for hard cases *(inference)*:
- A bilateral deficit with a one-sided condition is independent on the uninjured side at least; program it for both legs if the stage allows, or for the uninjured leg only (cross-education) if it does not [T26].
- An asymmetry whose weaker side is **not** the injured side is independent of the condition; say so in the rationale, because it changes the reading of the report (performance-tests.md on the direction of asymmetry).
- A deficit that could be either (for example a low DSI in an athlete with patellar tendinopathy in both knees) is treated as explained by the condition until the stage allows the test to be re-done without pain; the rationale says it is unresolved.
- On a Hamstring or ACL report the condition and side are fixed by the report and the rehab phase stands in for the stage; the sorting still applies to any performance-screen style numbers on the report (sprint, Nordic, hop).

## 3. Expected deficits by condition (what the injury itself lowers)

| Condition | Qualities the condition lowers on that side | How long | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| ACL reconstruction | Quadriceps isometric strength (ES −0.78 vs other leg, −0.76 vs controls); voluntary activation in both legs; jump and hop performance and symmetry (concentric impulse, concentric force, eccentric deceleration asymmetries persist beyond 9 months, d 0.42–1.35); landing mechanics | Years for strength and activation; >9 months for jump kinetics | T18, T38 |
| Hamstring strain | Isometric strength and straight-leg raise early (resolve in 20–50 days); eccentric and concentric knee-flexor strength and ratios after return to play; sprint mechanics | Weeks for isometric; months after RTP for eccentric | T19, T13 |
| Achilles tendinopathy | Plantar-flexor concentric strength (−38 to −44%), eccentric (−18%); hopping reactive strength (−16 to −35%); rate of force development (−10 to −21%) | While symptomatic and beyond | T20 |
| Patellar tendinopathy | Isometric (g 0.54) and concentric (0.78) knee-extensor strength; concentric knee flexion (0.41); probably hip strength; eccentric strength not consistently | While symptomatic | T21 |
| Lateral ankle sprain / instability | Dynamic balance, peroneal reaction time, eversion strength (strong evidence); proprioception and static balance (moderate) | Persistent in instability | T22 |
| Groin pain | Adductor squeeze strength at 45° (strong), total hip external rotation; adductor:abductor ratio (limited) | While symptomatic | T23 |
| Patellofemoral pain / OA | Hip strength in adults with PFP (likely a result of the pain rather than its cause); in patellofemoral OA hip abduction (SMD −0.96), extension (−0.72), external rotation (−0.55) and knee extension (−0.97), with smaller gluteal and quadriceps muscles (very low certainty) | While symptomatic | T24, T25 |
| Rotator cuff related shoulder pain, low back pain | See their guides; expect regional strength and endurance deficits rather than lower-limb performance changes | — | rotator-cuff.md, low-back-pain.md |

Reading the table: a quality in the row, on the condition's side, is "explained by the condition" in section 2. A quality not in the row (for example 30 m sprint time with patellofemoral pain) is independent unless the stage forbids the task.

## 4. Priority by stage: what leads, what runs alongside

| Stage (or rehab phase) | What leads | Performance section | Keep-up | Typical split of 4–8 exercises |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Early (protect and load; phase 1–2 after a muscle strain; early stage after ACL reconstruction) | The condition's early rows: pain-guided loading, isometrics, range, activation [T10] | Only what does not stress the tissue: the other limb hard (cross-education) [T26], trunk and hip, upper body, bike or pool conditioning for the heart and lungs [T10, T11] | Heavy work for untouched regions, 1–2 exercises [T27] | 3–4 rehab, 1–2 performance (other side / other region), 1 keep-up |
| Middle (build strength, capacity and control; mid-stage after ACL reconstruction) | Heavy slow or progressive loading of the tissue; strength of the whole limb (knee extensors and flexors, calf, hip) [T11] | Independent deficits trained fully; condition-side power still within the stage (no energy storage or sprinting unless the guide's row allows) | 1 exercise | 3 rehab, 2–3 performance, 1 keep-up |
| Late (energy storage, running, sport-specific; late stage) | The condition's progression becomes performance work: plyometrics, sprints, change of direction, built by the guide's criteria and the control–chaos continuum [T17, T42, T43] | The performance section and the rehab section merge: one power/reactive block that serves both; absolute strength kept heavy; the weaker side gets the extra set | — | 2 rehab-specific, 4–5 performance, with the condition's criteria as targets |
| Ongoing (maintenance) | Performance | Full program by the report's findings | The condition's maintenance dose (for example a weekly tendon loading or Copenhagen set) | 1 maintenance, 5–7 performance |

Where the physiotherapist's stage and the findings disagree (for example "late" stage but a 25% Nordic imbalance on the injured side), the findings pull the program back towards the middle stage for that quality and the rationale says so *(inference; rehab-principles.md rule 1)*.

## 5. Sections, headings and session order

- **Headings name the purpose.** With a condition: "Rehab: <condition>" (for example "Rehab: Achilles tendinopathy, middle stage"), "Performance: <deficits>" (for example "Performance: jump power and sprint"), and "Keep up" when used. Without a condition, headings name the intent of the block as before ("Power", "Strength").
- **Order in the session** follows the clinic guide's session order and the training-variables guide: the priority quality first; speed and power when fresh; heavy strength next; capacity, endurance and conditioning last [T28, T29]. The condition's loading goes where the clinic guide's order puts it: eccentric and isometric work after the strength lifts, or earlier when it is the main priority (isometric holds for tendon pain can open the session or precede sport); Copenhagen and calf capacity at the end. Nordic hamstring work can go before or after the session with the same strength gain; before training favours longer fascicles, after training favours thickness [T30].
- **Between sessions** keep ≥48 h between high-strain sessions for the same tendon or muscle (condition guides); separate heavy strength and conditioning by 3 h or do strength first (training-variables.md).
- **Progression inside the block** is written on the exercise: "weeks 1–3 double leg, from week 4 single leg"; "add 2.5 kg when all sets reach 8"; "from week 4 add the hop set if the 24-hour pain stays under 3/10". The instructions line on the handout stays consistent with these.

## 6. The blend over time: the rationale

Every suggestion with a condition carries a short rationale for the physiotherapist (never printed on the handout), in plain Australian English, 3–6 sentences:

1. **Priority:** which section leads this block and why (the stage, the pain response, the findings).
2. **The sort:** which findings were treated as the condition (named, with the number and side) and which as separate.
3. **The ceiling:** what was deliberately left out or kept light because of the stage (for example "no hops yet: tendon still painful on the 24-hour test").
4. **The next block:** what gets added or swapped and when, and the sign that moves it on — a pain level ("24-hour pain ≤3/10 on three hop sessions"), a symmetry ("hop RSI within 10% of the other side"), a test number ("Nordic imbalance under 10%"), a time floor ("not before month 9" after ACL reconstruction [T36]).
5. **Re-test:** what to re-test and roughly when (end of block or 4–6 weeks).

Why this matters: the evidence for progressing by criteria is strong for ACL reconstruction [T36, T37] and consensus elsewhere (rehab-principles.md); the return-to-sport continuum runs in parallel with rehabilitation [T6, T14]; and the one trial of deficit-driven, criteria-based programming shows fewer reinjuries [T13]. Writing the plan down is what lets the physiotherapist steer it.

## 7. Worked examples (how the rules combine)

- **Left Achilles tendinopathy, middle stage; screen shows low hop RSI left with a 28% reactive asymmetry, low DSI (0.55) with a high IMTP, 10 m sprint on target.** Sort: hop RSI and the asymmetry are the condition (reactive deficits of 16–35% are expected [T20]); the low DSI is independent (strength is high relative to jump: ballistic work is indicated, performance-tests.md). Program: Rehab — seated and standing heel-rise heavy slow loading (tendinopathy.md stage 2 rows), isometric holds at the start; Performance — loaded jump squats and medicine-ball throws for the ballistic deficit, but only double-leg and low-height until the tendon's energy-storage stage; Keep up — trap-bar deadlift heavy, one set. Rationale: tendon leads; hops return when the 24-hour pain rule is met for two weeks; re-test hop RSI at six weeks.
- **Hamstring strain, Return to Play phase (report); Nordic imbalance 22%, injured side weaker; 10 m sprint 0.08 s slower than the pre-injury result.** Sort: both are the condition (eccentric deficits persist after RTP [T19]; sprint mechanics are part of the injury [T13]). Program: Rehab — Nordic progression and eccentric sliders at the end of the session, sprint mechanics drills early (performance-oriented rehab, as in the algorithm trial [T13]); Performance — the uninjured qualities from the report (for example a heavy squat pattern for both legs); Rationale: imbalance under 10% and pain-free maximal sprints open the next block; the Nordic imbalance is the number to re-test fortnightly.
- **ACL reconstruction, 9 months (report); quad ISO LSI 84%, CMJ concentric asymmetry 15%, hop RSI both legs low.** Sort: the operated-side quad and jump asymmetries are the condition [T18, T38]; bilateral low RSI is independent (both legs). Program: Rehab — heavy unilateral knee-extensor and leg-press work with the operated side first and an extra set; Performance — bilateral reactive work within the acl.md late-stage rows (pogo and low drop jumps), building to single-leg when quadriceps LSI is ≥90% and peak torque above 2.5–3.0 Nm/kg (acl.md's late-stage row); Keep up — hip thrust. Rationale: the time floor of 9 months is met but the symmetry criteria are not [T36, T37], so the program stays late-stage, not return-to-performance; re-test quad LSI and concentric impulse asymmetry in six weeks.
- **No condition chosen; low DSI, low IMTP, slow 30 m.** No rehab section; the performance-tests and training-variables guides decide: strength first (low absolute force), sprint-specific practice for maximal velocity, one ballistic exercise; headings by intent.

## 8. Adjuncts and what to avoid

- Keep aerobic conditioning through rehab in a form the tissue allows (bike, pool, upper-body) [T10, T11] *(consensus)*.
- Cross-education and blood-flow restriction are the tools for a limb that cannot yet be loaded heavily [T26; rehab-principles.md rule 8].
- Avoid: programming the performance section as if the condition were not there; two exercises for the same quality on the injured side (one in each section); any later-stage row to "fix" a deficit faster; chasing a 10–15% asymmetry flag without checking its direction and the test; resting the athlete entirely while the tissue heals [T33].
- Do not use the acute:chronic workload ratio to decide loads [T32].

## 9. Red lines

- A red line in any condition guide (for example a suspected rupture, a locked knee, a stage the guide says needs medical review) stops the whole program and goes to notes, not just the rehab section.
- Energy-storage, sprinting and change-of-direction work for the injured tissue only at the stage the condition guide allows; pain-free for sprints and return-to-sport tasks (rehab-principles.md rule 4).
- Return-to-sport criteria are quoted as targets, never as a clearance; the decision is the physiotherapist's with the athlete and coach [T6, T12].
- Never infer a condition, a side or a stage from the numbers; if none is given, the program has no rehab section.

## 10. Evidence confidence and gaps

- Strong: expected deficits after ACL reconstruction and in Achilles and patellar tendinopathy [T18, T20, T21]; criteria and the nine-month floor after ACL reconstruction [T36, T37]; strength before endurance in a session [T28, T29]; the maintenance dose [T27]; the harm of load spikes and of under-training [T33, T35].
- Moderate: deficit-driven, criteria-based programming over a general protocol (one RCT, hamstring) [T13]; cross-education as an adjunct (consensus, small pooled effect) [T26]; Nordic placement [T30]; persistence of jump asymmetries after ACL reconstruction (cross-sectional) [T38].
- Weak or absent: any trial comparing rehab-first with rehab-plus-performance programming; periodised rehabilitation (scoping review: poor, inconsistent reporting) [T16]; the deficit tables for groin pain, ankle instability and PFP (mostly cross-sectional) [T22–T25]; the stage split in section 4 and the sorting rules in section 2 are this guide's inference and should be edited by the clinic.
- Frameworks without abstracts on record (optimal loading, PEACE and LOVE, tissue capacity, the progression models) are cited for their existence and well-known content only [T4, T5, T7, T8, T9].
