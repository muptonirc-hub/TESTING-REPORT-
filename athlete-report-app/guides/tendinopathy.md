# Tendinopathy: patellar in depth; Achilles, gluteal, proximal hamstring and lateral elbow (BASE Health Noosa guide library)

**Status: draft 1, 2 October 2026, written from published evidence for the clinic to review and edit.** Sources at the end with DOI/PMID (each checked against its Europe PMC record); tags like [T2] point to them. *(consensus)* marks expert agreement, *(practice)* a published clinical programme rather than a trial.

**Scope and use.** Exercise-based management of load-related tendon pain: how to stage it, what to load and how much, the pain rules, how to progress to energy-storage work and sport, and what not to do. Patellar tendinopathy is covered in depth because of the clinic's jumping athletes; Achilles (midportion and insertional), gluteal, proximal hamstring and lateral elbow follow. The physiotherapist makes the diagnosis and sets the stage; Claude drafts the program for that stage, using `training-variables.md` for general dosing and `rehab-principles.md` for the cross-cutting loading and pain rules.

---

## 1. Rules that matter most

1. **Load, don't rest.** Complete rest is not indicated and prolongs recovery; continue activity within pain tolerance and build tendon load progressively [T9, T10]. Athletes kept training and competing in-season while a 4-week isometric or isotonic program cut their pain [T6].
2. **The mode of loading matters less than doing it.** Heavy slow resistance, eccentric, progressive tendon-loading and moderate-load programs cannot be separated in network meta-analyses (low certainty); 90% and 55% of 1RM gave the same gains at equal volume; HSR equalled eccentric in the Achilles; isometric equalled isotonic for pain [T1, T2, T6, T17, T18, T31]. Choose by tolerance, stage and adherence.
3. **Progressive tendon-loading exercise is the best-tested patellar program**: isometric → isotonic → energy storage → sport-specific, 24 weeks, beat a fixed eccentric program by 9 VISA-P points (28 vs 18; 95% CI 1–16), with a trend to more athletes back in sport (43% vs 27%) [T25]. It has not been compared with HSR [T1].
4. **Isometrics relieve pain for some, not all.** Single bouts (5 × 45 s at 70% MVC) reduced patellar pain for at least 45 minutes in six athletes [T26] and gave more immediate relief than isotonic work over 4 weeks in-season [T27], but only 3 of 13 RCTs across tendons found a benefit and isometric was not superior to isotonic for chronic tendinopathy [T21, T38]. Use them as a pain-relieving entry point and in-season tool, not a cure.
5. **Pain rules are expert models, not tested cut-offs**: about ≤5/10 during and after exercise early, ≤3/10 as load builds, ≤1/10 in unrestricted sport; no worse the next morning; if pain rises week to week, reduce [T3, T10, T14]. Morning stiffness is the tendon's load monitor [T10].
6. **Treat structure as a risk factor, not the target.** Pain-free tendons carry pathology; symptoms improve long before (and sometimes without) structural change [T15, T18, T20]. Strength, flexibility and jump gains did not predict VISA-P change in the JUMPER trial [T5], so progress by symptoms and function, not imaging.
7. **Energy-storage loads provoke; introduce them last.** The single-leg decline squat (5.2 body weights), run-and-cut (5.5 BW), hops (4.6–4.7 BW) and running (4.8 BW) load the patellar tendon far more than a 60° squat (1.8 BW) or step-up (1.6 BW); the Spanish squat has a low peak but the highest impulse [T4]. Do not start rehab with the decline squat [T4].
8. **Build tendon capacity with heavy, slow loads**: tendon stiffness responds to loading magnitude (≥70% MVC, about 5% strain, 3 s contractions), not contraction type, and programmes of ≥12 weeks do more (see `training-variables.md`).
9. **Expect months, not weeks.** Patellar symptoms average 19 months and recur; Achilles relief begins at about 2 weeks and VISA-A improves 18–21 points by 12 weeks; gluteal tendinopathy success 77% at 8 weeks; return phases run 3–6 months with full recovery 12 months or more [T3, T9, T10, T11].
10. **Corticosteroid injection buys weeks and costs the year**: worse 12-month outcomes and 54% recurrence in lateral elbow pain [T33], deterioration by 6 months in patellar tendinopathy [T28], inferior to education plus exercise at 8 and 52 weeks in gluteal tendinopathy [T11], and worse 52-week function when added to resistance training [T19].
11. **Adjuncts add little**: shockwave added nothing to eccentric exercise for patellar tendinopathy, PRP effects are small or unproven, laser and ultrasound should not be used alone [T2, T9, T31].
12. **Return to sport needs symmetry and tolerance, not a date**: heel-rise endurance ≥90% of the other side and 20 single-leg hops at ≤2/10 for the Achilles [T10]; completion of sport-specific plyometrics with pain ≤1/10 for the patellar tendon [T3]; strength at least equal to the other side for the proximal hamstring [T14]. No validated patellar cut-off exists [T16].

## 2. Models and classification that change exercise choice

- **Terminology**: "tendinopathy" is the umbrella term for persistent load-related tendon pain and loss of function; imaging is not required for the diagnosis; a tear is a macroscopic discontinuity [T7]. Nine core outcome domains include pain on loading, participation, function, physical capacity and psychological factors, so track pain-on-load *and* a capacity measure [T8].
- **Continuum model** (reactive → disrepair → degenerative; not strictly sequential): reactive presentations are load-sensitive with a normal deeper tendon and settle with load reduction; degenerative tendons have mechanically silent regions and limited reversibility, so the aim is capacity of the whole tendon and muscle, not repair; "reactive-on-degenerative" is common in athletes [T15]. Early reactive tendons: avoid heavy eccentric work and intratendinous injection; degenerative tendons: progressive loading [T15].
- **Patellar**: pain at the inferior pole that rises with knee-extensor demand, worst with energy-storage tasks; diagnosis is clinical [T37]. Prevalence 18% in athletes (volleyball 25%, basketball 21%), 0.1% in the general population; adults > adolescents [T22]. Risk factors are weakly evidenced: greater activity and jump-training volume, higher body weight, reduced dorsiflexion and quadriceps/hamstring flexibility [T30].
- **Achilles**: midportion (2–6 cm above the insertion) versus insertional; insertional tendinopathy is compressed in dorsiflexion, so load from the floor with a heel lift and avoid step-edge heel drops [T9, T10].
- **Gluteal**: compression of the gluteus medius/minimus tendons in hip adduction (standing on one leg with the hip dropped, crossing legs, side-lying) drives it; mostly mid-life women [T35].
- **Proximal hamstring**: deep buttock pain with sitting, lunging and sprinting; compression plus tensile load at the ischial tuberosity; no RCT of rehabilitation yet [T13, T14].
- **Lateral elbow**: wrist-extensor tendinopathy; high recurrence; the 2022 guideline covers assessment and interventions [T12].

## 3. Stages and criteria

| Stage | Goal | Loading | Progress when | Typical time |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Symptom control (reactive or irritable) | Reduce pain, keep the athlete active | Isometrics 5 × 30–45 s at ~70% MVC, mid-range (knee 60°; Spanish squat; leg-extension hold), daily to several times a day; load management (reduce, not stop, energy-storage work) | Pain ≤5/10 during and after and settling by the next day; isotonic work tolerated | 0–4 weeks [T3, T6, T26] |
| 2 Isotonic strength (heavy slow) | Rebuild tendon and muscle capacity | 3–4 sets of 15 → 6 reps over 8–12 weeks, 3 s up / 3 s down, 2–3 min rest, 3 sessions/week (or every second day); 55–90% 1RM both work | Pain ≤3/10 during and next day; strength approaching the other side | Weeks 2–12 [T3, T18, T28] |
| 3 Energy storage | Restore the spring | Progressive jumping/landing: bilateral → unilateral, low → high intensity, every third day; hops and decline squats only now | Pain ≤2–3/10 and no morning flare; tolerates sport-specific volumes | Weeks 4–12+ [T3, T4, T14] |
| 4 Sport-specific and return | Full training, then competition | Sport drills, cutting, full jumping volume; 2–3 maintenance strength sessions/week continue | Minimal symptoms during loading, progressive strength, completed sport-specific plyometrics, pain ≤1/10 in sport [T3]; Achilles: heel-rise symmetry ≥90%, 20 hops ≤2/10 [T10] | 12 weeks to 6 months; maintenance ongoing [T3, T10, T25] |

In-season athletes can stay in stage 1–2 while competing: both isometric and isotonic programs cut pain over 4 weeks without any change to training or match load [T6, T27].

## 4. Exercise selection by stage

- **Isometric (stage 1)**: leg-extension hold at 60° knee flexion, Spanish/Catalan squat, wall sit; 5 × 45 s at 70–80% MVC, 15 s to 2 min rest, up to daily [T6, T26]. Achilles: isometric heel-rise holds; gluteal: isometric abduction in neutral; proximal hamstring: isometric hip extension/bridge holds 5 × 30–45 s up to 4×/day [T14].
- **Isotonic / heavy slow (stage 2)**: patellar — leg extension, leg press, squat, hack squat (Kongsgaard: 4 sets, 6-s repetitions, 15RM → 6RM over 12 weeks, 3 sessions/week) [T3, T28]; moderate load (55% 1RM) at equal volume works as well as 90% [T18]. Achilles — double then single-leg heel rises (straight and bent knee), seated heel rises, then weighted single-leg heel rises 3 × 15 adding load when easy; midportion over a step edge, insertional from the floor with a heel lift [T10]. Gluteal — 4–6 hip-abductor exercises daily with progressive load and dynamic adduction control; no compressive stretching [T11, T35]. Proximal hamstring — hip extension and bridge variants 2–3 × 10–15 progressing to 4–5 × 6–8, 2 s up / 3 s down, 3×/week [T14]. Lateral elbow — isometric, concentric and eccentric wrist-extensor loading (grade B); add shoulder/scapular work if impaired [T12].
- **Eccentric programs** (decline squat for the patellar tendon, Alfredson heel drops for the Achilles) remain effective first-line options; they are not superior to HSR or combined concentric–eccentric programs and adherence is lower [T17, T28, T31, T32].
- **Energy storage (stage 3)**: patellar — Spanish squat with impulse, double-leg jumps and landings, then single-leg hops, decline squats with speed, bounding; Achilles — quick-rebounding heel rises 3 × 20 then bilateral to unilateral hopping and skipping [T4, T10]; proximal hamstring — split squats and sprint drills 3 × 8–20, 2–3 days/week, pain ≤2/10 [T14].
- **Kinetic chain**: hip abductors and extensors, calf, trunk and quadriceps for proximal hamstring and patellar presentations; shoulder for the elbow [T12, T14, T15]. Strength training with an external cue or metronome may also address the altered cortical control seen in tendinopathy (tendon neuroplastic training) [T36].
- **Compression stages**: for the proximal hamstring, re-introduce hip flexion deliberately (modified deadlift, lunge, step bridge) once stage 2 is solid [T14]; for gluteal tendinopathy, teach pelvic control in single-leg stance and avoid hip adduction postures [T35].

## 6. Pain and load rules

- Working ceiling: ≤5/10 during and after exercise in stages 1–2 provided next-day pain is below 5/10; ≤3/10 during and next day in stage 3; ≤1/10 in unrestricted sport; if pain rises week to week, step back [T3].
- Achilles pain-monitoring model: light activities 1–2/10 during and next day, no recovery day; medium 2–3/10 during (3–4 next day), 2 recovery days; high 4–5/10 during (5–6 next day), 3 recovery days; athlete logs daily pain and perceived tendon exertion; avoid moderate–severe pain and any worsening trend [T10]. Continuing to run and jump under this model did no harm in a randomised trial (see `rehab-principles.md`).
- Proximal hamstring: ≤4/10 during exercise (≤2/10 in energy storage); a latent rise of ≤2/10 at 12–24 h is acceptable, ≥3/10 means reduce load or change the exercise [T14].
- Load management: cut energy-storage volume rather than stop (a clinical scheme allows <25% of sport volume in weeks 0–4, <50% weeks 2–6, <75% weeks 4–12, 100% from week 12) [T3]; in-season, keep training and compete while loading daily [T6, T27]; insertional Achilles and gluteal tendons: remove compression (heel lift; avoid adduction) [T10, T35].
- Education on load, pain and expectations is part of treatment (grade B for Achilles; the active ingredient with exercise in the LEAP trial) [T9, T11].

## 7. Return to sport and outcomes

- Patellar: no validated cut-offs; criteria in use are minimal symptoms with progressive loading, strength gains, completed sport-specific plyometrics and pain ≤1/10 in sport [T3, T16]. VISA-P improvement of ≥13–14 points is clinically meaningful (MDC about 11) [T5, T29]; 76% of athletes reached it at 24 weeks in the JUMPER trial, yet only 43% (PTLE) and 27% (eccentric) had returned to sport [T5, T25]. Symptoms average ~19 months; long-term cohorts show residual pain and a high rate of sport cessation [T3, T16].
- Achilles: heel-rise test symmetry ≥90%, 20 single-leg hops at ≤2/10, daily walking pain ≤2/10, sport-specific tests symmetric and compared with pre-injury baselines; return phase 3–6 months, full recovery ≥1 year; re-injury 27–44%, mostly after premature return [T10]. VISA-A gains 18–21 points by 12 weeks; both Alfredson and Silbernagel programs reach ~83–89/100 at 1 year with home-based delivery [T9, T32].
- Gluteal: education plus exercise gave 77% success at 8 weeks and 79% at 52 weeks versus 58% for injection and 29–52% for wait-and-see; pain 1.5/10 at 8 weeks [T11].
- Lateral elbow: PRTEE 7 points ("a little better") and 11 ("much better"); DASH 10; most recover by 12 months with physiotherapy or placebo, but corticosteroid injection halves 1-year recovery odds and raises recurrence to 54% [T12, T33].
- Proximal hamstring: strength at least equal to the other side over 3–6 months; return to running in 1-min intervals building to 20 min continuous before adding speed or hills [T14].

## 8. Adjuncts and what to avoid

- Corticosteroid injection: short-term relief, worse long-term outcome in lateral elbow (recovery 83% vs 96%, recurrence 54% vs 12%) [T33], deterioration after 12 weeks in patellar tendinopathy [T28], inferior to exercise in gluteal tendinopathy [T11], worse QuickDASH at 52 weeks when added to resistance training [T19]. Peritendinous injection into a reactive tendon is also discouraged on mechanistic grounds [T15].
- Shockwave: no benefit over sham when added to eccentric exercise for the patellar tendon [T31, T2]; short-term gains in Achilles and elbow that fade by a year [T24].
- PRP: small or unproven effects; the only pooled estimate available is low quality [T2, T23].
- Laser and ultrasound alone should not be used (Achilles grade C); orthoses no recommendation; heel lifts may help insertional pain temporarily [T9]. Topical GTN and hyaluronic acid are promising but single-trial [T31].
- Complete rest, stretching a compressed tendon (gluteal, insertional Achilles, proximal hamstring early), starting with decline squats or hops, and chasing imaging change are the common errors [T4, T9, T10, T15, T35].

## 9. Red lines

- Sudden loss of function, a palpable gap or inability to heel-raise or extend the knee suggests rupture: medical review.
- Do not inject or stretch a reactive, irritable tendon; do not prescribe energy-storage work before isotonic loading is tolerated [T4, T15].
- Progress by symptoms and function, never by a scan [T16, T20].
- Keep in-season athletes loading; do not stop them training to "rest the tendon" [T6, T9].

## 10. Evidence confidence and gaps

- Strong: loading beats rest and injections long term; HSR, eccentric, moderate-load and progressive programs are all effective; corticosteroid harm at 12 months; LEAP results for gluteal tendinopathy.
- Moderate: PTLE over eccentric (one RCT, 9-point advantage); isometric analgesia (small trials, mixed reviews); pain-monitoring models (expert, with one supporting RCT in the Achilles).
- Weak or missing: exact PTLE sets/reps and progression criteria (in the trial's supplement, not retrieved); validated return-to-sport cut-offs for the patellar tendon; recurrence rates; insertional Achilles trials; proximal hamstring RCTs (one under way); lateral elbow dosage; adolescents and elite jumpers.
- Populations: Achilles trials average 46 years, patellar 28, gluteal 55 (82% women); transfer to young athletes is by inference.
