# ACL reconstruction: rehabilitation and return to sport (BASE Health Noosa guide library)

**Status: draft 2, 2 October 2026, written from published evidence for the clinic to review and edit.** Sources at the end with DOI/PMID (each checked against its Europe PMC record); tags like [T1] point to them. *(consensus)* marks expert agreement; *(to verify)* a paper not opened in this pass. Figures were extracted from published pages by an automated reader; check any single number before it becomes a hard rule.

**Scope and use.** Programming after ACL reconstruction (and a note on non-operative care) for the ACL rehab tool, whose phases are Initial assessment, 3, 6, 9 and 12 months and 2 years, and whose return-to-sport checklist is the clinic's own. The physiotherapist sets the phase, interprets the tests and makes the return decision; Claude drafts within the phase given, using the dosing in `training-variables.md` and the loading and pain rules in `rehab-principles.md`.

## 1. Rules that matter most

1. **Criteria, not calendar.** Every guideline and consensus is criteria-based; no trial has validated any progression or discharge criterion (certainty very low to low) [T1, T2, T16, T17].
2. **Time is still a floor.** Young athletes returning before 9 months had 7 times the rate of new ACL injury (30% vs 4%; HR 6.7) [T4]; in the Delaware–Oslo cohort each month of delay up to 9 months cut reinjury by 51%, returning to pivoting sport carried 4.3 times the reinjury rate, and failing the test battery meant 38% reinjury versus 6% [T26]; the Panther and ISAKOS groups adopt the 9-month figure [T16, T17]. Plan 9–12 months [T2]; some argue for 2 years in the young, because baseline joint function is only reached about then and a third of young returners are reinjured within 2 years [T27].
3. **Early phase: move, load, activate.** Weight bearing as tolerated from week 1, no immobilisation, isometric quadriceps work from week 1–2, NMES early (moderate strength and large swelling effect), closed-chain from week 2–3, eccentric work from week 3 [T1, T2].
4. **Open-chain quadriceps exercise is safe when sequenced after closed-chain work**, from about week 4 in 90–45°, progressing toward full extension by weeks 6–9; it improves strength, pain and IKDC; laxity worsened only when open-chain work was done alone immediately after surgery; hamstring grafts need more caution [T1, T2, T10, T23, T25].
5. **Running gate** *(guideline)*: no or trace effusion, full extension, ≥95% flexion, quadriceps LSI >80%, countermovement-jump eccentric impulse LSI >80%, pain-free pool/AlterG jogging and single-leg pogo hopping; typically about 12 weeks, but 12 weeks alone is not a criterion [T1, T12]. In 201 studies the median permitted start was 12 weeks (range 5–39) and fewer than one in five used any clinical, strength or performance criterion; where used, these were full or >95% ROM, no or minimal pain, quadriceps and hamstring LSI >70% and hop LSI >70% [T28].
6. **Plyometrics and agility from >4 months** add benefit over usual care; eccentric plus plyometric training beats either alone; neuromuscular training accompanies strength work but does not replace it [T1, T2].
7. **Horizontal hop symmetry hides deficits.** Athletes cleared with 97% hop-distance symmetry had 83% single-leg vertical-jump symmetry, 77% single-leg drop-jump symmetry and 70–75% knee-work symmetry; use vertical jump height and reactive strength index [T3].
8. **Discharge for pivoting sport** *(guideline)*: no pain or swelling, full ROM, stable knee, isokinetic quadriceps and hamstring symmetry 100% at 60°/s, countermovement and drop-jump symmetry >90%, RSI >1.3 double-leg and >0.5 single-leg, running mechanics >90% symmetry, sport-specific training completed [T1]. Most groups use LSI 90–100% [T2, T17, T18].
9. **Batteries are rarely passed and are not a guarantee.** Pooled pass rate 33% (26% in non-professionals) [T5]; passing lowers graft rupture (RR 0.40) but not all ACL injuries and goes with more contralateral injuries (RR 3.35) [T6]; across four studies 43% passed and 14% of passers still had a second ACL injury, a non-significant 3% risk reduction (very low-quality evidence) [T29]. Failing the Delaware–Oslo criteria did predict reinjury (38% vs 6%) [T26]. Treat tests as readiness checks, not safety certificates.
10. **Measure psychological readiness** with a validated scale (mandatory in the ISAKOS consensus; ACL-RSI is standard in batteries); fear of reinjury is the main reason athletes do not return [T16–T18]. The 6-item short ACL-RSI is as robust as the full scale (6-month scores predict 12-month return, AUC 0.75–0.77) [T30]. The clinic's ACL-RSI threshold is not validated by any verified study.
11. **Blood-flow restriction** may help early strength when load is limited (pressures 40–80% of occlusion or 130–180 mmHg, 8–12 weeks); strength effects are mixed and bias is high [T1, T8, T9]. In the one UK NHS trial (n=24) low-load BFR matched heavy-load training for strength and muscle thickness while giving larger gains in function, balance and ROM and less pain and effusion [T31].
12. **Second injuries are common in the young**: overall second ACL injury 15% (7% graft, 8% contralateral), 21% under 25 years and 23% in under-25s who return to sport — 30–40 times the risk of uninjured adolescents [T32]; adolescents return to sport at 92% but graft rupture is 13% and contralateral rupture 14% [T21]; all re-ruptures in a 313-patient cohort were under 30 years [T22]; about 40% show radiographic osteoarthritis within 10–15 years [T24].

## 2. Classification and context that change the plan

- **Graft**: hamstring grafts are more vulnerable to early open-chain loading and to resisted knee flexion before week 12; bone–patellar tendon–bone grafts tolerate early open-chain quadriceps work with resistance but bring anterior knee pain and donor-site issues; return rates favour BPTB slightly (1.2×) [T1, T2, T4].
- **Age and level**: patients under 20–30 years and in high-demand pivoting sports carry the highest reinjury and battery-failure rates [T4, T21, T22]. Women return less often (men 1.4×) [T2].
- **Concomitant injury** (meniscus, cartilage, MCL) and surgery season change the timeline *(consensus)* [T16].
- **Pre-operative state**: a quadriceps deficit >20% before surgery predicts a 2-year strength deficit; an extension deficit raises complication risk; restore ROM and activation first and wait ~3 weeks after injury for inflammation to settle [T1, T2, T17]. Extended pre-operative rehabilitation (progressive strengthening and neuromuscular training, then criterion-based post-operative care) gave clinically meaningful better IKDC and KOOS scores and higher return-to-sport rates at 2 years than standard care in a cohort comparison [T33]; a systematic review of prehabilitation found better quadriceps strength, hop symmetry, self-reported function and return rates [T34].

## 3. Stages and criteria

| Stage (tool phase) | Goals | Entry/exit criteria | Typical window |
|---|---|---|---|
| Early (Initial → ~6 weeks) | Settle effusion, full extension, quadriceps activation, normal gait | Progressive weight bearing from week 1; active ROM at once; NMES; isometric quadriceps; CKC from week 2–3; OKC quadriceps from week 4 (90–45°); exit when gait is normal, extension full, effusion minimal | 0–6 weeks [T1, T2] |
| Strength (6 weeks → 3 months) | Strength, hypertrophy, control; begin running when criteria met | Running gate: no/trace effusion, full extension, ≥95% flexion, quadriceps LSI >80%, CMJ eccentric impulse LSI >80%, pain-free pogo hops and pool/AlterG jogging [T1]; clinicians commonly use pain ≤3/10 and swelling <grade 1+ [T20] | Running starts ~12 weeks (8–16) [T1, T12] |
| Power and agility (3 → 6 months) | Running volume, strength to ≥90% LSI, plyometric and change-of-direction progression | Plyometrics and agility from >4 months; COD 45° → 90° → 180°; eccentric + plyometric work; movement-quality cueing [T1, T2, T14] | 3–6 months |
| Sport-specific / on-field (6 → 9 months) | Sprinting to 90%+ of maximal velocity, reactive agility, contact drills, full training | Quadriceps LSI ≥90% and peak torque >2.5–3.0 Nm/kg *(narrative)* [T14]; on-field progression from controlled to chaotic tasks [T13, T14] | 5–9 months |
| Return to sport (≥9 months) | Clearance, return to performance, secondary prevention | Discharge battery in section 7; validated psychological scale; full sport-specific training completed; shared decision | ≥9 months; return to performance later [T1, T4, T16, T17] |
| Long term (12 months, 2 years) | Maintain muscle function and knee health | Physical parameters decline after discharge without maintenance; monitor effusion and pain; lifelong load management [T11, T19, T24] | ongoing |

## 4. Exercise selection by stage

- **Early**: isometric quadriceps and straight-leg raise; NMES; patellar and ROM work; early active motion; CKC from week 2 (leg press 0–45° from week 3 for hamstring grafts); eccentric cycle ergometer from week 3 (20–60° flexion); isokinetic hamstring work from week 3 for BPTB [T1, T2]. Avoid: immobilisation, CPM, whole-body vibration, dry needling very early, kinesio-tape as treatment [T1]. Cryotherapy for comfort in the first days, compressive better than ice alone [T1].
- **Strength**: open-chain knee extension from week 4 (90–45°, then 90–30° week 5, 90–20° week 6, 90–10° week 7, full by week 8–9; no added weight for hamstring grafts in the first 12 weeks [T2]); leg press, squat variations, hip hinge, calf; combine isotonic and isokinetic, OKC and CKC; eccentric with concentric [T1]. Progress loads beyond traditional protocols (from <50% toward >80% 1RM) once pain and effusion allow *(narrative)* [T19]. BFR when heavy loading is limited [T1, T8, T9]. Aquatic work from weeks 3–4 once healed [T1].
- **Power and agility**: submaximal plyometrics (skips, ladder, assisted pogos) then maximal, single-leg emphasis; drop jumps; sprint build 70% → 80% → 90% of maximal velocity over 3–4 weeks with 5–10 sets of 10–20 yd accelerations/decelerations; COD angles 45° → 90° → 180° with braking control [T14]; movement re-training in three stages (neuromuscular foundation → task re-learning → sport-specific field training) with external-focus cues [T13].
- **Sport-specific**: controlled opposition and contact drills, reactive agility, dual-task/decision-making work, position-specific match exposure built to pre-injury benchmarks [T13, T14, T19].
- **Return and maintenance**: continue strength and neuromuscular training; retest at intervals; address movement faults (dynamic valgus, hip internal rotation, shallow landing flexion) that predict reinjury [T2, T19].

## 5. Dosage

Guidelines give no sets, reps or %1RM for strength work after ACL reconstruction (very low-certainty evidence on dose) [T1]. Use `training-variables.md` with these constraints:

| Stage | Dose and limits | Sources |
|---|---|---|
| Early | Isometrics and NMES daily to several times a week; CKC 2–3 × 10–15 light; OKC 90–45° from week 4, no added load for hamstring grafts before week 12; eccentric cycle from week 3 | T1, T2 |
| Strength | Hypertrophy and strength dosing from the training-variables guide (moderate loads to 1–3 RIR; progress toward ≥80% 1RM); OKC sequenced after CKC; BFR 20–30% 1RM at 40–80% occlusion, 2–3 sessions/week, 8–12 weeks when loading is limited | T1, T8, T9, T10, T23 |
| Power | Plyometrics ≥8 weeks at any intensity were effective; 2–3 sessions/week, quality first; add eccentric work | T1 |
| Sport-specific | Sprint sets 5–10 × 10–20 yd, intensity rising 70→90% over 3–4 weeks; COD volume low and progressive | T14 |

Pain and effusion rule: progress only without an increase in effusion; running and jumping tasks pain-free [T1, T20].

## 6. Return to running and sport-specific running

- Typical start about 12 weeks; programme content (intensity, frequency, duration, progression) is poorly reported in the literature [T12].
- Criteria in use: guideline list above [T1]; physiotherapists most often use swelling, pain, knee-extensor strength and the single-leg squat with cut-offs of pain ≤3/10, swelling <grade 1+, LSI >70% [T20]; elite model: quadriceps ≥80% LSI at 3–5 months, pool/AlterG then on-field linear running [T14].
- Open-chain quadriceps work added from week 4 shortened time to running and improved LSI in a small cohort of footballers [T25].

## 7. Return-to-sport testing and decision

- **Guideline battery** [T1]: pain/swelling/ROM/stability; isokinetic quadriceps and hamstring 100% symmetry at 60°/s (pivoting sport); CMJ and drop-jump height and impulse >90% symmetry; RSI >1.3 (double) and >0.5 (single leg); running mechanics >90% symmetry; sport-specific training completed; subjective function and psychological readiness normalised.
- **Consensus**: abandon time-only decisions (100%); multidisciplinary (100%); objective exam (100%); standardised validated testing (88%); validated psychological scale (85–100%); consider sport, season and concomitant injury [T16, T17]. LSI 90–100% on strength and hop tests (88–96%); no consensus on hop-battery composition [T17].
- **What the tests predict**: failing the Delaware–Oslo battery (quadriceps strength, hop tests, KOS-ADLS and global rating all >90%) meant 38% reinjury vs 6% for passing, and more symmetrical quadriceps strength reduced reinjury [T26]; passing lowers graft rupture (RR 0.40) but not all ACL injuries, and raises contralateral injury (RR 3.35) [T6]; pooled across four studies the risk reduction was 3% and non-significant [T29]; in 313 patients passers re-ruptured 6.6% vs 10.3% (NS) and later [T22]; stronger quadriceps at 6 months went with *more* second injuries (exposure effect) [T7]; quadriceps symmetry did not predict new injury in young athletes [T4]. In 158 professionals, not meeting six discharge criteria carried 4 times the graft-rupture risk and a lower hamstring:quadriceps ratio raised it further (see `rehab-principles.md`).
- **The clinic's checklist** (quadriceps, hamstring and four hop LSIs ≥90, IKDC ≥90, ACL-RSI ≥65, ≥9 months) matches common practice [T18]. Evidence-based additions to consider: single-leg vertical jump and drop-jump height and RSI symmetry [T3], 100% isokinetic symmetry for pivoting sport [T1], the hamstring:quadriceps ratio (see `rehab-principles.md`), movement quality on landing and cutting [T2, T17], and comparing the injured limb with its own pre-injury or early-baseline values rather than relying on LSI alone (57% passed 90% LSI but only 29% passed 90% of estimated pre-injury capacity; see `rehab-principles.md`) [T1]. No ACL-RSI cut-off is validated; in athletes ≤20 years a score of 76.7 had 90% sensitivity for second injury in one cohort (see `rehab-principles.md`); the short 6-item ACL-RSI can replace the full scale [T30].

## 8. Timing, rates and second injury

- Return before 9 months: HR 6.7 for new ACL injury (10 of 33 vs 8 of 126) in 159 young athletes [T4]; 51% lower reinjury per month of delay up to 9 months as cited by consensus *(Grindem 2016 to verify)* [T16, T17].
- Return rates: 81% return to any sport, 65% to pre-injury level and 55% to competitive sport across 7,556 patients; younger age, male sex, elite level and a positive psychological response favour returning [T35]; elite athletes 83%, with graft rupture 5.2% and mostly unchanged performance [T36]; adolescents 92% any sport, 79% pre-injury level [T21]; ~65% of non-returners cite psychological reasons [T16].
- Second injury: 15% overall, 21% under 25 years, 23% in under-25s who return to sport, most early in the return period [T32]; adolescents ~13% graft and ~14% contralateral [T21]; young age, high activity level and low psychological readiness are the consistent risk factors [T4, T32; ACL-RSI data in `rehab-principles.md`].
- Secondary prevention: adding perturbation training to a strengthening, agility and secondary-prevention programme gave no extra benefit at 1 and 2 years in the ACL-SPORTS trial [T38]; the rationale for neurocognitive and visual-motor work is the altered brain activation seen after reconstruction [T39]; keep strength and neuromuscular training going after return [T1, T2, T19].
- Osteoarthritis: ~40% radiographic OA within 10–15 years; symptoms can appear within 5 years; OPTIKNEE recommends structured rehab, monitoring of muscle function and performance, and lifelong knee-health management [T11, T24].

## 9. Non-operative management

- Cross Bracing Protocol (80 consecutive patients, 12-month follow-up): 90% ACL continuity on 3-month MRI; better outcomes with grade 1 healing (Lysholm 98, return to sport 92%); re-rupture 14%; 2 deep-vein thromboses before prophylaxis; uncontrolled [T15]. Treat as promising, not proven.
- Rehabilitation-first management: in the KANON trial 30% of those randomised to optional delayed reconstruction showed MRI evidence of ACL healing at 2 years (53% of those never reconstructed), with better KOOS Sport/Rec and QOL than non-healed, delayed-reconstruction and early-reconstruction groups [T37]. Prehabilitation: moderate effect on post-operative quadriceps strength, none on hamstring strength or laxity [T1]; self-reported function and return rates improve [T2, T33, T34].
- Return-to-running criteria used by clinicians are the same for non-operative and operative patients [T20].

## 10. Red lines

- No return to sport before 9 months without the physiotherapist's explicit decision; no return with pain, effusion or instability [T1, T4, T16].
- No open-chain quadriceps loading before week 4, none with added load for hamstring grafts before week 12, and never as the only early exercise [T1, T2, T23].
- No immobilisation or CPM; no BFR without screening for cardiovascular disease and extensive swelling [T1].
- Do not clear anyone on time, LSI or a single test alone; add vertical-jump, movement-quality and psychological measures [T1, T3, T17].
- Report a worsening effusion, locking, giving way, calf pain and swelling, or fever to the surgeon or doctor.

## 11. Evidence confidence and gaps

- Strong and consistent: criteria over time; early loading and NMES; OKC safe after CKC; 9-month timing in the young; vertical jump over horizontal hop; low pass rates.
- Weak: exercise dose (none in guidelines); NMES parameters; running programme content; neurocognitive dose; secondary-prevention programmes; PROM MCIDs; ACL-RSI cut-offs; sex- and graft-specific rehab. Most evidence is low or very low certainty and male-dominant.
- Not verified in this pass: Perriman 2018 JOSPT open- vs closed-chain meta-analysis (superseded by the 2024 and 2025 analyses above); the 2024 BJSM risk-factor meta-analysis attributed to Ardern. Kyritsis 2016, Wellsandt 2017, Harput 2019 (cross-education), Hauger 2018 (NMES) and the Buckthorpe on-field papers are cited in `rehab-principles.md`.
