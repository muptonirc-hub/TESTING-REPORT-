# Designing the block: one focus, the week by training day, and what to defer (BASE Health Noosa guide library)

**Status: draft 1, 3 October 2026, written from published evidence for the clinic to review and edit.** Sources at the end with DOI/PMID (every source checked against its Europe PMC record); tags like [B2] point to them. Items marked *(practice)* are common coaching practice without a trial behind them; *(inference)* are this guide's own reasoning.

**Scope and use.** How a 4–8-week block is given one focus rather than a little of everything, how the week is laid out when the plan says 2, 3 or 4 sessions, how many exercises each day carries, and how the findings that are not the focus are kept or deferred. It applies to every suggestion. The clinic's programming guide (sections 5 and 7) sets BASE's own weekly structure and block lengths and wins where they differ; rehab-performance.md decides what leads when a condition is present; training-variables.md gives the dose for each quality; performance-tests.md says which quality a flagged result points to.

---

## 1. Rules that matter most

1. **One leading focus per block, chosen in a fixed order.** (a) A condition or injury that explains results on the report leads (rehab-performance.md); (b) otherwise the largest deficit in the quality the sport needs most, with the rule that a weak athlete builds strength before power and a strong athlete with a low DSI or RSI trains ballistic and reactive work (performance-tests.md); (c) at most one secondary quality; everything else is kept at a maintenance dose or deferred to the next block and named. Block-periodised training concentrates the work on a minimal number of compatible targets and develops qualities consecutively, where "mixed" training aimed at many abilities at once gives conflicting responses, more fatigue and a weaker stimulus per quality [B1, B2]; block periodisation has a single-goal form for individual sports and a multiple-goal form for team sports, and periodised, well-programmed training beats non-periodised [B4]. A program that focuses on the least-developed quality gains most (rehab-performance.md, Cormie 2011).
2. **The evidence for a single focus is strongest for power, speed-strength and jump, and for sequencing strength before power.** Block training beat traditional and mixed-session training for power and vertical jump at equal volume (jump +7.2% vs +1.2% over 10 weeks; a 79.8% likelihood of a better force–power curve over 15 weeks) and gained more strength per unit of volume than daily undulating training in track-and-field athletes [B5, B6, B8]. It is **not** superior for maximal strength and muscle size: mixed sessions gained more bench strength and size than block in trained men (+8.6% vs +2%), weekly undulating beat block for lower-body strength in recreationally trained women (+27.7% vs +15.2%), and linear and undulating periodisation do not differ for strength across 17 studies [B7, B8, B10]. So the focus rule decides what comes first in the session, what gets the most sets and what is progressed; it does not ban the other qualities.
3. **A focus block for power still needs its strength base.** Stronger athletes improved jump faster under block training and relative strength predicted the rate of gain [B9]; in high-performance sport maximal strength needs habitual loads of ≥80% 1RM and >15-rep work is a poor stimulus [B3]. Keep one heavy lift in a power block; keep one ballistic exercise in a strength block *(practice)*.
4. **Treat the rule as planning discipline, not a law.** Periodisation research is short-term (nothing beyond nine months), mostly young trained men, rarely tests what the theory predicts and ignores how much responses vary between people [B11, B12, B13]; recovery, nutrition, sleep and skill work are periodised alongside the exercise plan in an integrated approach [B14]; block periodisation's small advantage for endurance (VO2max SMD 0.40) comes from low-quality studies [B22]. The block is a hypothesis the re-test checks; the next block's focus is chosen from the re-test, not from the plan.
5. **The number of days is the person's choice; the week's job is placement.** With weekly volume equated, 2 and 3+ sessions give the same strength and growth [B15, B16, B21], and adding a day is mainly a way to add volume for a trained lifter [B20]; full-body twice a week equalled an upper/lower split four times a week in untrained women [B17]; three consecutive days equalled days 48–72 h apart [B19]; in trained men total-body favoured strength and a split favoured growth [B18]. What the day split must do: give the focus quality the freshest slot on at least two days; keep heavy and high-strain work on the same tissue 48–72 h apart (72 h between a hypertrophy session and a strength-power session for the same muscles; plyometrics may follow strength-power the same day after ≥3 h) [B3]; spread the main lifts across the week (clinic guide section 5).
6. **Each day is a short session: 3–5 exercises, written out by day.** The focus quality gets its exercise first on its days; the same exercise may appear on two days with different loads (a heavier and a moderate day, as the clinic guide's two-day rule) *(practice)*; a day never carries two exercises for the same quality (clinic guide section 2).
7. **Deferred findings are named, not forgotten.** The rationale lists what was deferred and the block it belongs to; the sequence is strength (or rehab loading) → power/speed → sport-specific, with the stage rules of the condition guides deciding when rehab work becomes performance work (rehab-performance.md). Residual effects (how long a quality lasts untrained) are part of the block model [B1] *(durations to verify)*; a maintenance dose holds strength for months (rehab-principles.md).

## 2. Choosing the focus

| Situation on the report | Focus of this block | Secondary (at most one) | Kept or deferred | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A condition or injury explains the flagged results (same side, a quality it lowers at this stage) | The condition's loading at its stage | The largest independent deficit, within the stage's rules | Other deficits deferred and named; one heavy keep-up exercise | rehab-performance.md; B1, B2 |
| No condition; absolute strength low (IMTP relative force, quad ISO, main lifts Off target) | Maximal strength | One ballistic exercise to keep power ticking | Reactive and speed work light; re-test strength at the end of the block | B3, B9; performance-tests.md |
| No condition; strength adequate, power or reactive strength low (DSI low with high IMTP, RSI low, jump low) | Ballistic and reactive power | One heavy lift to keep strength | Capacity and endurance deferred | B5, B6, B8 |
| No condition; one-sided deficit with consistent direction (Nordic imbalance, adductor asymmetry, quad asymmetry) | The weaker side's quality (eccentric hamstring, adductor, unilateral strength) | The sport's main quality | Bilateral power work kept light until symmetry improves | performance-tests.md |
| Several equal deficits | The one the sport needs most, else the one with the largest gap to target; the others named as the next blocks | — | Deferred in order | B1, B2 *(inference)* |

## 3. The week by training day (defaults; the clinic guide's section 5 wins)

| Sessions | Layout | Where the focus sits | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | Two full-body days: A heavier, B moderate | Both days, first | Power at low volume both days; the same main lift on both with different loads [B17] *(practice)* |
| 3 | Day 1: power + main strength (freshest); Day 2: strength + hamstrings/hips or the rehab loading; Day 3: capacity and control + a light power touch | Days 1 and 2 first; Day 3 lighter | Keep plyometrics and sprints ≥48 h apart; the rehab section's heavy loading on Day 2 if Day 1 carried power [B3]; consecutive days are acceptable [B19] |
| 4 | Two lower-body days ≥48 h apart (one power-led, one strength-led) + two lighter days (capacity, control, upper body or the rehab section) | The two lower-body days, first | Plyometrics on 2–3 days at most, 48–72 h apart; 72 h between hypertrophy and strength-power sessions on the same muscles [B3] |
| New to training | The same 2–3 full-body sessions repeated, progressing one variable at a time | First on every day | Full-body twice a week matched a split in untrained women [B17]; learning the lifts matters more than variety early *(practice)* |
| Home setting | Days follow the same pattern with body weight, bands, a backpack and single-leg versions (clinic guide section 6) | — | — |

Exercises per day: 3–5. Total over the block: sessions × that, with the same exercise allowed on two days. Progression inside the block: one variable at a time (clinic guide section 7).

## 4. Evidence confidence and gaps

- Strong: frequency works through volume [B15, B16, B21]; linear and undulating periodisation equal for strength [B10]; the critiques of periodisation research [B11–B13].
- Moderate: block periodisation's advantage for power and jump and for efficiency in trained athletes (small trials) [B5, B6, B8, B9]; the 72 h hypertrophy↔strength-power spacing and ≥3 h strength-power→plyometrics gap [B3]; full-body vs split equivalence (one RCT in untrained women, one in trained men) [B17, B18].
- Weak or absent: any trial of focused vs spread blocks in injured athletes or in rehab-plus-performance programs; any test of day templates against a repeated session; residual-effect durations (not verified); the exact exercise count per day (practice).
