# Reading the Performance screen: what each measure means and the training it points to (BASE Health Noosa guide library)

**Status: draft 1, 3 October 2026 (the DSI wording revised the same day), written from published evidence for the clinic to review and edit.** Sources at the end with DOI/PMID (every source checked against its Europe PMC record); tags like [P6] point to them. Items marked *(practice)* are conventions in the testing literature without a validated cut-off; *(inference)* are this guide's own reasoning.

**Scope and use.** The Performance & Readiness screen reports composite and paired measures: DSI, RSI-modified and hop RSI, contact time, concentric, landing and reactive asymmetries, Nordic peak force and L/R imbalance, adductor and abductor force with their asymmetry and ratio, IMTP peak force, relative force and RFD 0–200 ms, quad ISO left and right, sprint splits and the 5-10-5. This guide says what a low, high or asymmetric value means, which training quality it points to, and which thresholds have evidence behind them, so that Claude picks the right exercise family for a flagged result and does not over-read a flag. The training-variables guide gives the dose for each quality; the clinic's programming guide gives BASE's own exercise families and wins where they differ; rehab-performance.md says what to do when a condition is also present. The norms file sets the On target / Close / Off target bands; nothing here replaces them.

## 1. Rules that matter most

1. **Read a ratio with its parts.** DSI is driven mostly by the IMTP (r −0.85 in men, −0.75 in women) and only weakly by jump force (0.30), jump height (0.11–0.17), RSI-modified (0.17–0.27) or RFD (−0.34); athletes with the same DSI can need different training [P5]. Say which part is low before choosing the exercise.
2. **Low DSI with high IMTP → ballistic and plyometric work; high DSI with modest IMTP → maximal strength.** Low-DSI athletes (0.55) produced the same jump propulsion force as high-DSI athletes (0.92) from a far higher IMTP force (46.7 vs 31.1 N/kg) with a deeper, faster countermovement [P2]; four weeks of strength training lowered DSI (0.71 → 0.65) by raising IMTP force, with the high-DSI group changing most (0.85 → 0.74) and the low-DSI group not at all [P4]. The 0.60 and 0.80 bands used in these papers are conventions, not validated cut-offs *(practice)*. On BASE's reports the DSI is not rated (no target, never good or bad): it sets the emphasis and the training variables, and any deficit lies in the CMJ power output or the IMTP force, never in the DSI itself.
3. **RSI is a reactive and change-of-direction quality, not a strength proxy.** Across 32 studies RSI correlated moderately with strength (r 0.34), endurance (0.40), acceleration (−0.43) and top speed (−0.33) and strongly with change-of-direction speed (−0.57) [P6]. A low hop RSI or long contact time points at stretch-shortening-cycle work (pogo, drop jumps, hopping) and at change-of-direction drills, once the tissue allows, more than at heavy strength.
4. **RSI-modified is explosiveness from a countermovement jump** (jump height ÷ time to take-off), loading on both force and speed [P7]; a low value with a good jump height is a slow strategy (train rate of force development and intent), a low value with a low jump height is a power problem (strength base plus ballistic work) *(inference)*.
5. **Asymmetry flags are prompts, not diagnoses.** Of 18 studies applying a threshold, 15 used 10–15% without evidence for it; asymmetry ranges from near zero to >15% within healthy groups; associations with injury and performance are inconsistent [P12]; 8 of 31 injury studies found no link, 10 partial, 10 significant, at moderate-to-low quality [P13]; strength asymmetries may hurt jumping and kicking, jump asymmetries versus change of direction are mixed, and there are no trials [P10, P11]. Check the size, the direction and the test before treating it.
6. **Direction matters and changes between tests.** The side an asymmetry favours was only fairly to substantially consistent between sessions (Kappa 0.29–0.66) while its size was stable [P14], and asymmetries rarely favoured the same side across an isometric squat, a single-leg jump and a broad jump [P15]. An asymmetry whose weaker side is the known injury side is the injury; one that points the other way is a separate finding, and one that flips between tests may be noise *(inference)*.
7. **After ACL reconstruction, jump and landing asymmetries are a knee signal.** Concentric impulse, concentric peak force, eccentric deceleration impulse and eccentric RFD asymmetries persist beyond nine months (d 0.42–1.35) with lower jump performance than controls [P27]; frontal-plane knee movement, knee-extensor-moment asymmetry at landing and single-leg balance deficits predicted a second ACL injury (C statistic 0.94) [P26]. Program them through the ACL guide, not as generic power work.
8. **Nordic numbers: both the force and the imbalance carry risk, and the thresholds are sport-specific.** Elite AFL: eccentric force < 256 N at the start of preseason RR 2.7, < 279 N at the end RR 4.3, a >10% imbalance not a risk [P17]; elite soccer: < 337 N RR 4.4, short biceps femoris fascicles (< 10.56 cm) RR 4.1, high strength offsetting the extra risk of age and previous injury [P16]; rugby union: imbalance ≥ 15% RR 2.4, ≥ 20% RR 3.4, previous hamstring injury RR 4.1, absolute force not a risk [P18]. Nordic-based programmes halve hamstring injuries (RR 0.49) [P19]. Low force → eccentric knee-flexor strength (Nordic progression, slider curls, long-length work); imbalance → the same with the weaker side first and an extra set *(practice)*.
9. **Adductor force and the adduction:abduction ratio point at groin risk.** Hockey players with adduction below 80% of abduction were 17 times more likely to strain an adductor (78% vs 95% in the injured and uninjured) [P20]; professional footballers who went on to a groin injury had lower isometric adductor force and larger asymmetries, with force predictive and asymmetry not reaching significance [P21]; prevention programmes cut groin injuries by a non-significant 19% in 2015 [P22], and the Copenhagen adduction programme later by 41% (groin-pain.md). Low adductor force or a low ratio → progressive adductor loading (Copenhagen progression, side-lying and standing adduction); a low abductor number → hip-abductor strength.
10. **IMTP: peak force is the strength base; RFD is the speed of it.** Isometric force-time variables relate small-to-large to jumps, sprints, change of direction and weightlifting (r² 0.09–0.94 across 47 studies) [P23]. Low relative peak force → maximal strength; low RFD 0–200 ms with adequate peak force → fast-intent strength, ballistic and plyometric work (training-variables.md on RFD).
11. **Sprint splits and the 5-10-5 are different qualities.** The change-of-direction deficit (505 time minus 10-m time) isolates turning ability from speed: it correlated with 505 time (0.74–0.81) but not with sprint time, and reclassified 5 of 17 athletes [P24]. A slow 5-10-5 with a good 10 m is deceleration, re-acceleration and technique; a slow 10 m is acceleration (horizontal force, strength); a slow 30 m with a good 10 m is maximal velocity (sprint practice). Sharper and faster cuts load the knee more and are progressed by angle and approach speed, especially after injury [P25].
12. **Quad ISO left and right are read against the condition.** After ACL reconstruction the operated leg is weaker (ES −0.78) with activation deficits in both legs for years [P29], and normal recovery curves at 3, 6 and 9 months differ by sex and graft (hamstring-tendon grafts show higher early relative torque and symmetry than patellar- or quadriceps-tendon grafts) [P28]; patellar tendinopathy and patellofemoral pain lower knee-extensor strength on the painful side (rehab-performance.md section 3). Without a condition, a one-sided quad deficit with a jump asymmetry the same way is a strength asymmetry to train unilaterally; both low is a strength base to build.

## 2. Measure by measure

| Measure | What it is | Low or flagged value points to | Watch-outs | Sources |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Jump height, peak power (CMJ) | Ballistic output | Power work on a strength base; check DSI and IMTP to see which | Strategy (depth, speed) changes height without changing capacity | P2, P5 |
| CMJ peak force | Force in the propulsion phase | With low IMTP: strength; with high IMTP: ballistic intent | Similar across DSI groups; not a strength measure on its own | P2 |
| RSI-modified | Height ÷ time to take-off | Explosiveness: fast-intent strength, ballistic and plyometric work | Valid in one female volleyball sample; a slow strategy lowers it | P7 |
| Concentric asymmetry (CMJ) | L/R difference in propulsion | Unilateral strength and power on the weaker side; after ACL reconstruction, the knee's rehab | Thresholds arbitrary; direction varies between tests | P12, P14, P15, P27 |
| Landing asymmetry (CMJ) | L/R difference on landing | Landing mechanics and eccentric control; after ACL reconstruction, a second-injury signal | A knee signal more than a performance one | P26, P27 |
| IMTP peak force, relative force | Maximal strength | Maximal strength training | Dominates the DSI; relative force is the comparable number | P5, P23 |
| IMTP RFD 0–200 ms | Speed of force | Fast-intent strength, ballistic and plyometric work (if peak force is adequate) | 200-ms RFD is largely explained by maximal strength | P23; training-variables.md |
| Hop RSI, contact time, hop jump height | Reactive strength (fast stretch-shortening cycle) | Pogo, drop jumps, hopping; change-of-direction drills | RSI's strongest link is to COD speed; low RSI predicted injury only in female volleyball (≤ 0.91 m/s, RR 4.2) | P6, P8 |
| Reactive asymmetry | L/R difference in hop RSI | Single-leg reactive work, weaker side first; after knee injury, a late-rehab marker | Reactive asymmetry (22%) persisted into the final RTS phase when strength was symmetrical | P9 |
| Nordic peak force L/R, imbalance, relative force | Eccentric knee-flexor strength | Nordic progression, slider and long-length eccentrics; weaker side first | Thresholds 256–337 N by sport; imbalance ≥15% (rugby); previous injury multiplies risk | P16–P19 |
| Adductor and abductor force, adduction asymmetry, Add:Abd ratio | Hip adductor and abductor strength | Adductor loading (Copenhagen progression); abductor strength; ratio < 0.80 is the historic flag | Small cohorts; ratio evidence from hockey | P20–P22 |
| DSI (CMJ ÷ IMTP) | Ballistic force relative to maximal force | Not a deficit: sets the emphasis. ≳0.80 with modest IMTP: strength; ≲0.60 with high IMTP: ballistic/plyometric; between: both | Not rated on BASE's reports; bands are conventions; read with the parts | P1–P5 |
| Quad ISO 60° L/R | Knee-extensor isometric strength | Unilateral knee-extensor strength (leg extension, split squat, leg press); after ACL reconstruction, the ACL guide's targets | Activation deficits in both legs after ACLR; recovery curves differ by graft | P28, P29 |
| 10 m, 20 m, 30 m | Acceleration → maximal velocity | 10 m: horizontal force and strength, resisted sprints; 30 m: maximal-velocity sprinting | Separate qualities; see training-variables.md T44–T46 | P24; training-variables.md |
| 5-10-5 | Change of direction | Deceleration and re-acceleration strength, COD technique and progression by angle and speed; reactive strength | Use the COD deficit idea: compare with the 10 m | P24, P25 |

## 3. From a flagged result to an exercise family

- **One exercise per quality.** Low CMJ power output and a low RSI-modified in the same athlete are one power problem, not two; pick one ballistic exercise and one strength exercise, not two jumps (clinic guide).
- **Strength base first when absolute force is low** (IMTP relative force Off target): heavy multi-joint strength leads; ballistic work is light and technical until the base exists (rehab-performance.md rule 7; training-variables.md).
- **Ballistic and reactive work when maximal force is high and power output or reactive strength is low** (good IMTP with low CMJ power or RSI; a DSI ≲ 0.60 points the same way): loaded jumps about 30% 1RM, weightlifting derivatives, plyometrics with short contacts; strength kept with one heavy lift [P2, P4, P5].
- **Asymmetry work only when the direction is consistent with the other tests or with a known injury**; otherwise train bilaterally and re-test [P14, P15]. When treating it, the weaker side goes first and gets the extra set *(practice)*.
- **Speed**: acceleration deficits → horizontal-force work (resisted sled sprints 10–20% body mass, hill sprints) and strength; maximal-velocity deficits → maximal sprints at ≥95% with full recovery (training-variables.md).
- **Change of direction**: build deceleration strength and reactive strength, then COD technique at increasing angles and approach speeds; after injury, start with shallow angles and slow approaches [P25].
- **Nordic and adductor flags** are injury-prevention findings as much as performance ones: the Nordic programme halves hamstring injuries [P19] and adductor strengthening cuts groin injuries (groin-pain.md), so they earn a place even in a performance-led program.

| Measure | Threshold | Evidence | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nordic eccentric force (AFL) | < 256 N start of preseason; < 279 N end | RR 2.7 and 4.3 for hamstring injury | P17 |
| Nordic eccentric force (soccer) | < 337 N | RR 4.4; fascicles < 10.56 cm RR 4.1 | P16 |
| Nordic L/R imbalance (rugby) | ≥ 15% / ≥ 20% | RR 2.4 / 3.4; previous HSI RR 4.1 | P18 |
| Adduction:abduction ratio (hockey) | < 0.80 | 17 × adductor-strain risk; injured 78% vs uninjured 95% | P20 |

## 5. Pain, load and conditions

- A flagged result on the side of a known condition is the condition's business (rehab-performance.md sections 2–3): expected deficits include quadriceps strength and jump asymmetry after ACL reconstruction [P27, P29], plantar-flexor and reactive strength in Achilles tendinopathy [P30], eccentric knee-flexor strength after a hamstring strain, knee-extensor strength in patellar tendinopathy and patellofemoral pain.
- Reactive and plyometric work for a tendon or a healing muscle only at the stage its guide allows; sprinting pain-free (rehab-principles.md).
- Load progression for the performance work follows the training-variables guide; avoid rapid jumps in plyometric volume or sprint volume (rehab-performance.md rule 10).

## 6. Return to sport and outcomes

- Reactive asymmetries can persist into the final return-to-sport phase when strength is symmetrical [P9]; CMJ kinetic asymmetries persist beyond nine months after ACL reconstruction [P27]; functional tests predict return to performance only weakly (rehab-performance.md T39). Use the screen to set targets and re-test, not to clear.

## 7. Adjuncts and what to avoid

- Avoid treating a single asymmetry flag as an injury-risk diagnosis [P12, P13]; avoid reading DSI without IMTP and CMJ values [P5]; avoid two exercises for the same quality; avoid assuming a slow 5-10-5 is slow sprinting [P24].

## 8. Red lines

- Never infer an injury or a side from the screen; a landing asymmetry after ACL reconstruction goes to the ACL guide and the physiotherapist [P26].
- Never use the screen's numbers as a clearance.

## 9. Evidence confidence and gaps

- Strong: RSI's associations by task [P6]; the asymmetry literature's inconsistency and arbitrary thresholds [P10–P15]; Nordic force and imbalance as risk factors and the Nordic programme's effect [P16–P19]; persistent asymmetries and quadriceps deficits after ACL reconstruction [P26–P29].
- Moderate: DSI interpretation (small training and cross-sectional studies) [P1–P5]; adductor ratio (one small cohort, 2001) [P20, P21]; the COD deficit (one small study, widely adopted) [P24].
- Weak or missing: validated DSI and asymmetry cut-offs; RSI norms by sport and sex; Nordic relative-force norms *(to verify)*; evidence that training to a screen result changes injury rates (none for most measures).
