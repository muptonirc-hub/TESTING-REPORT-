# Low back pain in active adults and athletes: exercise-based management (BASE Health Noosa guide library)

**Status: draft 1, 2 October 2026, written from published evidence for the clinic to review and edit.** Sources at the end with DOI/PMID (each checked against its Europe PMC record); tags like [T1] point to them. *(consensus)* marks expert agreement; *(practice)* a standard clinical convention without a trial behind it; *(inference)* a working rule that no study tested; *(to verify)* a number read from a secondary summary that should be checked against the paper before it is relied on.

**Scope and use.** Non-specific low back pain (LBP) in active adults and athletes: the acute episode, the recurrent pattern and persistent (>12 weeks) disabling pain, plus prevention of recurrence. Radicular pain (sciatica) is touched on only where guidelines include it. The physiotherapist screens for serious pathology and radiculopathy, assesses psychosocial factors, stratifies risk and sets the stage; Claude drafts the exercise program for the stage given, using `rehab-principles.md` for the cross-cutting rehab rules and `training-variables.md` for sets, reps, load and progression. Medication, imaging, injections and surgery are medical decisions.

---

## 1. Rules that matter most

1. **Almost all LBP is non-specific, and most episodes settle.** In 1,172 acute primary-care presentations a specific cause was found in 0.9% (fracture the commonest) [T1, to verify]; imaging findings are as common in people without pain [T1]; most new episodes improve quickly, but recurrence is common and about half of primary-care patients run a continuing or fluctuating course [T1]. High initial pain, psychological distress and pain at multiple sites predict persistence [T1].
2. **First-line care is education, staying active and exercise; imaging, rest, opioids, injections and surgery are over-used.** The Lancet series, 15 primary-care guidelines and 11 high-quality guidelines across musculoskeletal pain say the same thing: screen red flags, assess psychosocial factors, image selectively, educate, address physical activity and exercise, use manual therapy only as an adjunct, exhaust good non-surgical care before surgery and keep people at work [T2, T3, T15]. The ACP guideline makes non-pharmacological care the strong first choice for acute, subacute and chronic LBP [T16].
3. **For persistent LBP, exercise works and the type matters less than doing it.** Against no treatment, usual care or placebo, exercise cut pain by 15.2/100 (95% CI 12.2–18.3; moderate certainty, clinically important) and functional limitation by 6.8/100 across 249 trials [T17]; it beat education alone by 12.2/100 [T17]. Network meta-analyses rank Pilates, McKenzie and functional restoration (pain) and flexibility (function) highest (−15 to −19/100 pain vs minimal treatment) [T18], or Pilates for pain and resistance and motor control for function [T19], but all active types beat control and the authors advise prescribing the exercise the person will keep doing [T18]. Motor control exercise is not clinically better than other exercise [T20]; aerobic and resistance training reduce pain equally (SMD −0.42), with resistance adding a mental-health gain [T24].
4. **For an athlete, bias toward posterior-chain resistance training for 12–16 weeks.** Against general exercise or walking, posterior-chain resistance training (hip hinge, deadlift and hip-lift patterns) produced larger gains in pain (SMD −0.61, 95% CI −1.21 to 0.00), disability (−0.53) and strength (+0.67) with no more adverse events, and the effect was clearer at 12–16 weeks than at 6–8 [T21]. Deadlift training helped most those with lower pain and disability and better Biering-Sørensen endurance at baseline [T23, pre-2016]; for people who score poorly there, low-load motor control plus education gave a better activity outcome (PSFS +4.2 vs +2.5) with equal pain and strength change [T22, pre-2016]. Sequence: control and endurance first when needed, then load *(inference from T22, T23)*.
5. **Dose: about 20–30 minutes, 3 sessions a week (up to 5), for at least 12–16 weeks.** Study-level analyses converge on sessions of 15–30 minutes [T5, T6, T8], three sessions a week [T6, T8] (frequency was the strongest predictor of effect in core-training trials, 3–4 a week [T7]), and programs of 16 weeks or more [T5, T6]; trials themselves averaged 7 weeks [T8]. These are between-trial comparisons, not dose-randomised evidence, and were read from summaries *(to verify)*.
6. **Treat the whole person when pain is disabling: cognitive functional therapy has the strongest single trial.** In 492 adults with chronic disabling LBP, up to seven physiotherapist sessions over 12 weeks plus a booster at 26 weeks (making sense of pain, exposure with control, lifestyle change) reduced Roland-Morris disability by 4.6 points (95% CI 3.4–5.9) versus usual care at 13 weeks, held at 52 weeks, with lower societal cost; movement-sensor biofeedback added nothing [T9]. Gains ran through pain self-efficacy, fear of movement, catastrophising and pain intensity [T10]. Pooled CFT trials show low-to-moderate certainty effects on disability and pain and high certainty for self-efficacy [T34]; comparison with well-delivered exercise is not settled [T12, T34].
7. **Don't oversell education in the acute phase.** Adding 2 hours of intensive patient education to first-line care for acute LBP did not improve pain at 3 months (−0.3/10) and gave only a 1.6–1.7-point Roland-Morris advantage at 1 week and 3 months that was gone by 6 months [T28]. Brief reassurance, advice to stay active and a plan to return to sport are the education that matters early [T15, T16, T31] *(inference)*.
8. **Lifting with a flexed spine is not the villain.** Across 11 studies, greater lumbar flexion during lifting was not a risk factor for LBP onset or persistence, and people with LBP lifted with 6.0° less flexion than people without (low-quality evidence) [T25]. Teach confident, varied lifting and build capacity rather than enforcing a rigid neutral spine *(inference)*; avoid language about fragile or unstable spines, which the Lancet series flags as part of the problem [T2].
9. **Prevent the next episode with exercise, with or without education, not with belts or insoles.** Exercise plus education cut the risk of a new LBP episode (RR 0.55, 95% CI 0.41–0.74; moderate quality), exercise alone RR 0.65; education alone, back belts and insoles did nothing [T26]. Exercise programs reduce future pain intensity (MD −4.5/100) and exercise-plus-education reduces future disability (MD −6.3/100) [T27]. A progressive walking program with six physiotherapist sessions over 6 months lengthened the median time to an activity-limiting recurrence from 112 to 208 days (HR 0.72) and was cost-effective [T13].
10. **Athletes: same rules, thinner evidence.** Lifetime LBP prevalence in athletes ranges 1–94% (highest in rowing and cross-country skiing), point prevalence 18–65% [T30]; 14 RCTs in 541 athletes show exercise reduces pain and disability but none reports return-to-sport data [T29]. The rowing consensus adds: educate on risk factors, technique and training load; unload early from the aggravating activity while keeping fitness; control pain; exercise; progress load back to full training [T31] *(consensus)*.
11. **Re-assess at 4 weeks.** Guidelines advise referral or re-assessment when there is no improvement after about 4 weeks of first-line care, or when specific pathology or radiculopathy is suspected [T15]; track progress with a disability score and pain rating against the minimal important change (RMDQ 5 points, ODI 10 points, NRS 2 points, or 30% from baseline) [T33, pre-2016].

## 2. Classification and assessment that change the plan

- **Triage** *(consensus)*: (1) serious spinal pathology (fracture, malignancy, infection, cauda equina) — rare, about 1% of acute presentations [T1]; use the red-flag reasoning framework rather than single flags, most of which lack diagnostic accuracy [T32]; (2) radicular syndrome by neurological examination [T15]; (3) non-specific LBP, the rest [T1, T15].
- **Duration** *(practice)*: acute <6 weeks, subacute 6–12 weeks, persistent ≥12 weeks (the Cochrane and NMA reviews define chronic as ≥12 weeks) [T17, T18].
- **Risk stratification**: NICE advises a tool such as STarT Back at first contact to match care intensity [T4, archive copy]; psychosocial factors (fear, catastrophising, low self-efficacy, distress, work issues) are assessed in every high-quality guideline [T3] and were the mediators of CFT's effect [T10].
- **Capacity and load**: Biering-Sørensen (trunk extensor endurance) and lumbopelvic control predict who tolerates heavy lifting rehabilitation early [T23]; record pain intensity (NRS), disability (RMDQ or ODI) and a patient-specific functional scale [T22, T33]; record training load history and the aggravating activity for athletes [T31].
- **Imaging**: not routine; only when serious pathology is suspected or it would change management [T2, T4, T15].
- **Prognostic flags**: high initial pain, distress, multi-site pain [T1]; a history of more than two previous episodes marks the recurrent pattern (used for stratification in WalkBack) [T13].

## 3. Stages and criteria

Timings and criteria are a template drawn from guideline advice and the trial programs *(inference from T9, T13, T15, T21, T31)*.

| Stage | Goal | Content | Progress when | Typical time |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Acute (0–2 weeks) | Reassure; stay active; protect sleep; keep fitness | Brief education on favourable prognosis; continue normal activity, avoid bed rest; unload the aggravating sport action while keeping general and cross-training load; walking; gentle movement in preferred directions; GP for analgesia if needed | Pain settling (NRS down ≥2 or 30%); walking and sitting tolerance improving; no neurological change | days to 2 weeks [T1, T15, T16, T31] |
| 2 Subacute (2–6 weeks) | Rebuild capacity; start the exercise habit | Supervised exercise 2–3/week, 20–30 min: trunk endurance and control, hip and posterior-chain strengthening at tolerable load, aerobic work; graded return to the sport action; address fear and beliefs | RMDQ/ODI improving toward MIC; sport action tolerated at reduced volume; Biering-Sørensen and hinge pattern competent | weeks 2–6 [T5–T8, T22, T33] |
| 3 Persistent or slow (>6–12 weeks) | Reduce disability; restore full training | 12–16-week program, 3/week: posterior-chain resistance (hinge, deadlift variants, hip thrust) progressed by load; or Pilates/motor-control/aerobic by preference; CFT-informed sessions (up to 7 over 12 weeks + booster) when fear, distress or avoidance dominate; group exercise option | ≥30% improvement in disability and pain; full training session tolerated; strength and endurance targets met | 12–16+ weeks [T9, T17, T18, T21] |
| Return to sport and prevention | Compete; avoid recurrence | Keep 2–3 strength/exercise sessions a week; progressive walking or aerobic habit; load-management plan and education; booster review at 6 months | Full competition without activity-limiting recurrence; review at 3, 6 and 12 months | ongoing [T13, T26, T27] |

If there is no improvement after about 4 weeks of appropriate care, re-examine, re-screen for red flags and radiculopathy, review psychosocial factors and consider referral [T15].

## 4. Exercise selection by stage

- **Acute**: movement people tolerate and enjoy; walking; keep the rest of the body training [T13, T16, T31]. Superficial heat, massage and spinal manipulation are acceptable short-term comforts in the ACP guideline (low-to-moderate quality) [T16] but NICE restricts manual therapy to packages with exercise [T4].
- **Subacute**: build the pattern library — hip hinge, squat, carry, lunge, bridge and side plank — at loads inside the pain rule; trunk extensor endurance toward a normal Biering-Sørensen hold; low-load motor control first for people who move poorly or hurt a lot, because that group did better with it than with heavy lifting [T22]; simple aerobic work [T24].
- **Persistent, athlete or strength-oriented person**: posterior-chain resistance training as the backbone for 12–16 weeks [T21]; deadlift training is appropriate once pain and disability are modest and extensor endurance is adequate [T23]; resistance training improves function and mental health [T19, T24].
- **Persistent, person who prefers movement classes**: Pilates ranked first for pain in two NMAs [T18, T19]; McKenzie and functional restoration also ranked high in one [T18] while McKenzie did not beat control in the other [T19]; motor control is as good as other exercise and as good as manual therapy, not better [T20]; tai chi and yoga are endorsed by the ACP [T16]. Choose by preference and adherence [T18].
- **Persistent, fear or distress dominant**: a CFT-informed approach — make sense of pain, graded exposure to feared movements with control strategies, lifestyle change (sleep, activity) — delivered over up to seven sessions in 12 weeks with a booster [T9, T10]; CBT-based therapy within a package when psychosocial barriers block progress [T4].
- **Prevention**: any exercise program, with education, continued after recovery [T26, T27]; individualised progressive walking with six coaching sessions over 6 months in the one large trial [T13].

| Core training sub-analyses | Resistance 3–4/week, 30–45 min; Pilates 2–3/week, 50 min, 8–12 weeks; core stability 3–4/week, 40–60 min, 6–8 weeks; frequency the strongest predictor of pain effect | T7 (to verify) |

| Aerobic or resistance training | Supervised ≥1/week, performed ≥2 days/week for ≥6 weeks in the pooled trials | T24 |
| Deadlift rehabilitation | 8 weeks supervised by a physiotherapist with powerlifting experience; suitable when pain and disability are lower and Biering-Sørensen endurance higher | T23 |

## 6. Pain and load rules

- **No trial-tested pain threshold exists for LBP exercise.** Apply the acceptable-pain rule from `rehab-principles.md` (pain acceptable during and after exercise if it settles to baseline by the next morning and function is not worsening) *(inference)*; exposure to feared but safe movements with control strategies is part of the treatment, not a hazard [T9, T10].
- **Unload the aggravating action, not the person**: reduce or modify the specific sport action early while keeping general training and fitness [T31]; return to it in graded volume *(inference)*.
- **Lifting**: lumbar flexion during lifting is not a risk factor; do not prescribe a rigid neutral spine as a safety rule [T25]; progress hinge loading like any other strength exercise once extensor endurance and pain allow [T21, T23].
- **Progression**: raise load or volume weekly within the strength rules of `training-variables.md`; 12–16 weeks is the horizon for strength-based programs [T21]; expect pain and disability to improve by 30% (MIC) within the first 4–6 weeks of a well-run program, and re-examine if they do not [T15, T33].
- **Adverse events**: exercise programs carried no more adverse events than general exercise [T21]; walking programs raised lower-limb complaints (100 vs 54 events) without changing total adverse events [T13]; motor control trials reported minor or no adverse events [T20].

## 7. Return to sport and outcomes

- **No return-to-sport criteria have been tested in athletes with LBP** [T29]. Working criteria *(practice)*: disability and pain at or near normal (RMDQ ≤4 and NRS ≤2, or ≥30% improvement and stable) [T33]; full training sessions tolerated at competition intensity without a next-day flare; trunk endurance and hinge strength at the clinic's norms; the athlete reports confidence to perform the aggravating action [T10, T31].
- **Expected change**: exercise versus control −15/100 pain and −7/100 function at first follow-up [T17]; Pilates, McKenzie or functional restoration −15 to −19/100 pain [T18]; CFT 4.6 Roland-Morris points better than usual care at 13 and 52 weeks [T9]; motor control vs minimal intervention −13/100 pain long term [T20].
- **Minimal important change** [T33, pre-2016]: VAS 15/100, NRS 2/10, RMDQ 5/24, ODI 10/100, or 30% from baseline for any of them.
- **Recurrence**: median 112 days to an activity-limiting recurrence with no treatment versus 208 days with a walking-and-education program [T13]; exercise-based prevention roughly halves the odds of a new episode (RR 0.55 with education, 0.65 alone) [T26].

## 8. Adjuncts and what to avoid

- **Acceptable adjuncts**: manual therapy (manipulation, mobilisation, soft tissue) only within a package that includes exercise [T3, T4]; superficial heat and massage for acute pain comfort [T16]; psychological therapies (CBT, mindfulness-based stress reduction) for persistent pain, within a package [T4, T16]; multidisciplinary rehabilitation for persistent disabling pain [T16].
- **Disagreements to know**: acupuncture is recommended by the ACP [T16] and not offered by NICE [T4]; spinal manipulation is an option in the ACP guideline [T16] and an adjunct only in NICE and the 11-recommendation synthesis [T3, T4].
- **Do not offer** (NICE NG59, read from an archive summary, to verify): belts or corsets, foot orthotics, rocker-sole shoes, traction, ultrasound, TENS, PENS, interferential therapy, acupuncture, paracetamol alone, routine opioids, SSRIs/SNRIs or anticonvulsants for LBP, spinal injections for non-specific LBP, disc replacement [T4]. Back belts and insoles also fail as prevention [T26].
- **Low-value care named by the Lancet series**: routine imaging, bed rest, opioids, spinal injections and surgery for non-specific LBP [T2]. Medication, when used, is a GP decision: NSAIDs first-line, opioids only after other options fail [T16].

## 9. Red lines

- **Serious pathology screening every visit** *(consensus)*: use the red-flag reasoning framework — history, risk factors and the evolving clinical picture, not single flags [T32]. Urgent referral for: bladder or bowel disturbance, saddle anaesthesia or progressive bilateral leg symptoms (cauda equina); significant trauma, osteoporosis or long-term steroid use with new pain (fracture); history of cancer, unexplained weight loss, constant night pain (malignancy); fever, recent infection, immunosuppression or intravenous drug use (infection); progressive neurological deficit *(practice)*.
- **Radicular pain with weakness or progressive neurological signs**: medical review before loading [T15] *(practice)*.
- **Adolescent or young athlete with extension-loaded sport (cricket, rowing, gymnastics, fast bowling) and focal extension pain**: consider a bone stress injury of the posterior elements and arrange medical assessment before extension loading *(practice)*; LBP affects about 40% of 9–18-year-olds, so most is still non-specific [T1].
- **No improvement after 4 weeks of appropriate care, or deterioration**: re-examine, re-screen, review psychosocial factors, consider referral [T15].
- **Psychosocial red lines** *(inference)*: high fear, catastrophising or distress should change the program toward a CFT-informed approach and, if needed, psychological referral, rather than more or heavier exercise [T9, T10, T34].

## 10. Evidence confidence and gaps

- **Guideline level**: strong convergence on first-line care and low-value care [T2, T3, T4, T15, T16]; the 2021 APTA guideline exists but its specific recommendations were not read [T14]; NICE was read from an archive copy and its 2020 update was not checked [T4].
- **Exercise versus control**: moderate certainty for a clinically important pain effect, below-threshold function effect [T17]; which type is best is unsettled and NMAs disagree on McKenzie [T18, T19]; motor control is not superior [T20]; dose findings are ecological and were extracted from page summaries *(to verify)* [T5–T8].
- **Posterior-chain and deadlift training**: 8 trials, n = 408, borderline pain effect (upper CI 0.00) [T21]; deadlift predictors come from one 35-person cohort [T23]; the LMC-vs-lifting trial is pre-2016 [T22].
- **CFT**: one large well-run trial against usual care [T9, T11] and pooled trials of low-to-moderate certainty [T34] (an earlier pooled analysis rated certainty very low with high heterogeneity [T12]); superiority over well-delivered exercise is untested.
- **Athletes**: 14 small, biased RCTs; no return-to-sport data [T29]; sport-specific guidance is consensus-based (rowing) [T31].
- **Prevention**: exercise works [T26, T27]; the walking dose in WalkBack was not read from the paper [T13].
- **Pre-2016 items** flagged in Sources: T22, T23, T33 (used for predictors and minimal important change only).
