# Rotator cuff related shoulder pain, cuff tears and repair, and the overhead athlete (BASE Health Noosa guide library)

**Status: draft 1, 2 October 2026, written from published evidence for the clinic to review and edit.** Sources at the end with DOI/PMID (each checked against its Europe PMC record); tags like [T1] point to them. *(consensus)* marks expert agreement; *(practice)* a published program without comparative trial evidence.

**Scope and use.** Exercise-based management of rotator cuff related shoulder pain (RCRSP: subacromial pain, cuff tendinopathy, partial and atraumatic full-thickness tears), rehabilitation after rotator cuff repair, and the overhead or throwing athlete's shoulder. The physiotherapist makes the diagnosis and decides on referral; Claude drafts the program for the stage given, using `rehab-principles.md` for the cross-cutting loading and pain rules and `training-variables.md` for general dosing.

---

## 1. Rules that matter most

1. **Exercise first, and it matches surgery.** A graduated exercise approach gives at least equivalent benefit to surgery for subacromial pain, cuff tendinopathy, partial tears and atraumatic full-thickness tears [T18]. Decompression surgery was no better than placebo arthroscopy at 6 and 12 months (Oxford Shoulder Score −1.3 and +0.3) [T3], nor at 24 months in the Finnish trial [T22]; the Cochrane review and a BMJ guideline panel recommend against it [T23, T32]. Repair of small atraumatic supraspinatus tears in people over 55 was no better than physiotherapy at 1 year or 6 years, physiotherapy alone treated about 75% of atraumatic full-thickness tears for 2 years, and the Cochrane review of cuff repair found no placebo-controlled trial and little difference from non-operative care [T10, T11, T20, T33].
2. **Progressive, resisted exercise is the active ingredient**: versus placebo or no treatment it improves composite pain and function by about 15 points per 100 (low certainty), while non-progressive or unloaded exercise does not (4 points, NS) [T7]; exercise beats non-exercise controls for pain (SMD −0.94) and specific exercise beats generic (SMD −0.65) [T26]; exercise is a strong first-line recommendation with manual therapy as an adjunct, and laser, shockwave, ultrasound and electromagnetic therapy have no effect [T24]; clinical practice guidelines agree on exercise, education and activity modification as the essentials [T36].
3. **More physiotherapy is not better than a good self-managed program.** In 708 patients, up to six sessions of progressive exercise did no better over 12 months than one best-practice advice session with a self-managed exercise ladder (SPADI difference −0.7), and corticosteroid injection added nothing (−1.1) [T1]; a single-exercise self-managed program equalled usual physiotherapy [T19]; adding a large dose of strengthening to usual care changed nothing, and about half of patients still had unacceptable symptoms at 4 months [T20a]; adding motor-control or strengthening exercises to education did not beat education alone [T27]. Prescribe a few well-chosen, progressed exercises and coach self-management.
4. **Load intensity is not a settled lever**: progressive high-load (15RM → 6RM) and traditional low-load (20–25RM) training gave the same DASH change over 12 weeks (−1.4 points difference) [T8]; strengthening programs do improve external-rotation strength (SMD 0.56) and other directions (0.41–0.48), and passive treatments do not [T5]. Strength gains are often small and the benefit of exercise is broader than mechanics; frame it beyond "you are weak" [T25].
5. **Pain during exercise is allowed if it settles**: some pain if manageable and settled within a few hours (GRASP) [T2]; "to but not through" pain at effort 4–6/10 (criteria-based cohort) [T9]; isometric holds as a fallback when pain exceeds 50/100 [T8]. No trial has compared pain-limited with painful loading in the shoulder.
6. **Essential exercises** *(GRASP)*: resisted external rotation, flexion and abduction, one to three exercises chosen with the patient, starting 1 × 8 and building to 12 reps then up to 3 sets at a Borg 3–4 effort, 5 days a week with 2 rest days, continued for at least 4 months [T2]. Typical trial programs run 2–3 sets of 8–20 reps, 2–5 sessions a week for 8–12 weeks [T7].
7. **Criteria, not calendar**: a 12-week three-phase program progressed by pain, range, strength and tolerance (not time), with 3 sets to technical fatigue at Borg 4–6, home ≥4 days a week, reached the pain MCID by 6 weeks and gained strength mainly between weeks 6 and 12; 11% still chose surgery by 26 weeks [T9].
8. **After cuff repair, early motion is safe and gains little**: early versus delayed mobilisation changes re-tear risk not at all (OR 1.05; NNH 651) and adds 1–8° of range in the first months, a finding repeated across overviews and reviews since 2016 [T12, T13, T21, T34, T35]; early active motion within 6 weeks did not compromise integrity (30% vs 33% full-thickness defects at 12 months) [T28]; large tears with early passive motion trended toward more re-tears (OR 2.07, NS) [T13]. Protect large tears longer; let small and medium tears move.
9. **Imaging describes the shoulder, not the pain**: most MRI abnormalities are equally common in the painful and the pain-free shoulder of the same person; only full-thickness supraspinatus tears and glenohumeral OA were about 10% more frequent on the symptomatic side [T29]. Glenohumeral OA progressed over 6 years whether or not the cuff was repaired [T10].
10. **Overhead athletes: screen external rotation, not GIRD.** Pre-season range-of-motion screening has limited predictive value; only external-rotation insufficiency in professional pitchers predicted injury (OR 1.9), while GIRD and total arc did not [T14, T15]. Risk rises with prior shoulder pain or injury, load spikes (acute:chronic ratio, >60% weekly increases), reduced external-rotation strength and an isometric ER:IR ratio below 0.75 [T16].
11. **Measure force where it matters**: the Athletic Shoulder (ASH) test is reliable (ICC 0.94–0.98; minimum detectable change 13–26 N) for tracking the ability to produce and transfer force across the shoulder girdle in athletes [T30]; combine analytical strength tests with functional performance tests for screening and return to play *(consensus)* [T31].
12. **Rehabilitate the chain**: scapular stabilisation improves pain and DASH [T6]; prior trunk or lower-limb injury (HR 2.6) and hip-abductor asymmetry (RR 1.24 per 5%) raise shoulder-injury risk in athletes [T16]; kinetic-chain work and sport-specific loading belong in the late stage *(consensus)* [T31].

## 2. Classification and assessment that change the plan

- **RCRSP** is the umbrella term for non-traumatic subacromial pain, cuff tendinopathy and partial tears [T18]; trials define it by pain with loaded elevation or rotation and positive clinical tests, often with ultrasound-confirmed tendinopathy (RoCTEx) [T8]. Special tests and imaging cannot localise the painful structure; manage by irritability and load tolerance [T18, T29].
- **Atraumatic full-thickness tears**: start with physiotherapy; about 75% avoid surgery over 2 years and those who do operate decide within 6–12 weeks [T20]; small tears (~10 mm) in people over 55 do as well with physiotherapy at 6 years [T10].
- **Traumatic tears, significant weakness, or younger patients with full-thickness tears**: surgical opinion (the trials above excluded traumatic and large tears) [T10, T11, T20].
- **Post-repair**: tear size sets the protection period — small and medium tears tolerate early motion; large tears get a longer protected phase [T12, T13].
- **Overhead athlete**: a throwing shoulder normally shows more external rotation, less internal rotation and more humeral retroversion than the other side; adaptation is not pathology. Flags: external-rotation insufficiency (throwing arm <5° more than the other) in pitchers, internal-rotation deficit >13° or horizontal-adduction deficit >15° in adolescent pitchers (4–6× risk), ER <93° or >100° in swimmers [T14, T15, T16].
- **Outcomes**: SPADI, DASH/QuickDASH (MCID ~10.5), WORC, Oxford Shoulder Score (MCID 4.5), Constant; pain rating (MCID ~1.4) [T3, T8, T9, T27].

## 3. Stages and criteria

| Stage | Goal | Content | Progress when | Typical time |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Calm and move (irritable pain) | Reduce fear and pain; restore movement | Education, activity modification, pain-free or tolerable mobility work, isometric holds if loading hurts (>50/100), light band external rotation/flexion/abduction | Pain manageable and settling within hours; near-full active range | 2–4 weeks [T2, T8, T9] |
| 2 Load the cuff | Progressive resistance of the posterior cuff and scapular muscles | 1–3 essential exercises: resisted external rotation, flexion, abduction; scapular stabilisers; 1 × 8 → 12 reps → 3 sets at Borg 3–6, 5 days/week home, supervised review every 1–2 weeks | Strength gains measurable; pain MCID reached (by ~6 weeks in the criteria-based cohort) | Weeks 2–12 [T2, T8, T9] |
| 3 Function and sport | Restore patient-specific tasks and sport loads | Compound pressing/pulling, kinetic-chain and trunk work, overhead and plyometric loading, throwing or sport-specific progressions | Task performance pain-free; strength symmetric (ASH or dynamometry); sport drills tolerated | Weeks 8–16+; continue ≥4 months [T2, T9, T31] |
| Post-repair (separate pathway) | Protect the repair, then restore | Sling 0–6 weeks (comfort-only for small/medium tears); passive range from week 0–3 (early) or 4–6 (delayed); active-assisted and active from ~6 weeks; resisted strengthening from ~12 weeks; sport-specific later | Pain-free active range before resistance; strength symmetry before overhead sport | 4–6 months to strength; return to overhead sport unverified [T10, T12, T13, T28] |

## 4. Exercise selection by stage

- **Essentials** *(GRASP)*: resisted external rotation, resisted flexion, resisted abduction with bands or hand weights; optional stretching; an 8-step difficulty ladder from passive movement to loaded full-range work [T2].
- **Scapular and posterior cuff**: scapular stabilisation (retraction, protraction, rows, serratus work) helps pain and function [T6]; both RoCTEx arms added two scapular-stabiliser and two mobility exercises [T8].
- **Eccentric, concentric, motor control**: no mode is reliably superior — concentric strengthening ranked best and high-load lowest in one network meta-analysis with implausible effect sizes [T4]; eccentric training helped mid-term function in another [T6]; motor control versus strengthening made no clinically meaningful difference [T27]. Choose by tolerance and the task deficit.
- **Isometrics**: fallback when loading pain is high [T8]; not superior to isotonic exercise across tendinopathies (see `tendinopathy.md`).
- **Kinetic chain and sport**: trunk and hip strength and control, then sport-specific throwing/serving/swimming progressions and plyometric upper-limb work *(consensus)* [T16, T31]; posterior-shoulder endurance was protective in swimmers [T16]; sleeper/posterior-capsule stretching for pitchers with internal-rotation deficits [T15].
- **Manual therapy**: a reasonable adjunct in the early phase, better than placebo and additive to exercise only at the shortest follow-up [T24, T26]. Narrative syntheses of current practice cover exercise parameters, scapular and kinetic-chain work, post-operative mobilisation and education [T17].

## 5. Dosage

| Program | Dose | Source |
|---|---|---|
| GRASP progressive exercise | ≤6 physiotherapy sessions over 16 weeks; 1–3 exercises; 1 × 8 → 12 reps → up to 3 sets; Borg 3–4 start; 5 days/week with 2 non-consecutive rest days; continue ≥4 months | T2 |
| GRASP best-practice advice (as effective) | One 60-min visit, booklet, 1–2 self-guided exercises on a progressive ladder, resistance band, "moderately difficult", 5 days/week | T1, T2 |
| RoCTEx | High-load 15RM (week 1) → 6RM (weeks 9–12) vs low-load 20–25RM throughout; 2 cuff + 2 scapular + 2 mobility exercises; home 3×/week; 5 supervised sessions in 12 weeks; same outcome | T8 |
| Progressive protocols in trials | 2–3 sets × 8–20 reps, 2–5 sessions/week, 8–12 weeks, bands or weights progressed by ability or pain tolerance | T7 |
| Criteria-based program | 3 phases over 12 weeks; 3 sets to technical fatigue, Borg 4–6; home ≥4 days/week; review every 1–2 weeks then less; reassess at 2, 6 and 12 weeks | T9 |
| Strength-focused programs | 6–12 weeks, 2–3 sessions/week; ER strength SMD 0.56 | T5 |
| Small-tear physiotherapy (Kukkonen) | Weeks 1–6 glenohumeral motion and scapular retraction; 6–12 progressive static/dynamic exercise; 12–24 resistance and strength; 10 supervised sessions | T10, T11 |
| Post-repair | Early passive motion within 2–6 weeks or delayed to 4–8 weeks; sling for comfort to 6 weeks; active from ~6 weeks; resisted from ~12 weeks | T12, T13, T28 |

## 6. Pain and load rules

- Some pain is acceptable if manageable and it settles to an acceptable level within a few hours [T2]; work "to but not through" pain at an effort of 4–6/10 and talk about discomfort rather than damage [T9]; if pain exceeds 50/100 during a loaded exercise, switch to an isometric hold [T8]. Older trials used pain-free or "no increased pain" rules; transient post-exercise increases occurred in about 30% of one trial's exercise group without harm [T7].
- Progress by pain, range, strength and tolerance, not by week; hold or regress when a session provokes [T2, T9].
- Education comes first and is itself effective; a stepped approach (education, then exercise for those who need it) is proposed [T27].
- Corticosteroid injection: short-term benefit over no treatment (SMD −0.65) [T26] but no advantage over 12 months and no additive effect with exercise [T1]; a possible interaction with high-load training is hypothesis-generating only [T8].

## 7. Return to sport and outcomes

- Expected course: pain MCID by about 6 weeks and strength gains by 6–12 weeks on a progressive program [T9]; DASH improves 7–8 points over 12 weeks in tendinopathy [T8]; about half still have unacceptable symptoms at 4 months in subacromial pain cohorts [T20a]; 11–16% of non-operative cohorts proceed to surgery within 6 months to 6 years [T9, T10].
- Overhead athletes *(consensus)*: combined analytical (isometric strength, ER:IR ratio, range) and functional tests (sport-specific performance, closed-chain upper-limb tests) before return; progressive sport-specific loading (interval throwing or hitting programs) with load monitoring [T31]. Thresholds seen in cohorts: isometric ER:IR <0.75 (OR 4.3) and eccentric ER <2.9 N/kg (OR 3.5) in handball; a handball prevention program halved injuries (HR 0.44) [T16].
- ASH test for tracking: reliable across I, Y and T positions; changes under 13–26 N are noise [T30].
- Post-repair return to overhead sport: no verified timeline; strength symmetry and pain-free sport-specific loading after the strengthening phase (from ~12–16 weeks) are the working criteria *(inference)*.

## 8. What to avoid

- Arthroscopic subacromial decompression for non-traumatic pain: no better than placebo surgery, complications include frozen shoulder; strong recommendation against [T3, T22, T23].
- Routine repair of small atraumatic tears in people over 55 [T10, T11]; routine imaging-led decisions [T29].
- Non-progressive, unloaded exercise as the whole program [T7]; passive modalities alone (laser, shockwave, ultrasound, electromagnetic therapy: no effect) [T24].
- Repeated corticosteroid injections for long-term benefit [T1]; prolonged immobilisation after repair of small and medium tears [T12, T13, T28].
- Treating strength as the only mechanism: gains are often small and clinically unimportant, so set expectations around function and confidence [T25].

## 9. Red lines

- Traumatic full-thickness tear with weakness, especially in younger or active patients; suspected dislocation or fracture; progressive weakness or wasting; night pain with systemic features; neurological signs → medical or surgical review.
- Post-repair: no resisted loading before the surgeon's protocol allows; large tears protected longer [T13].
- Overhead athletes: do not clear on range of motion alone; load spikes are the modifiable risk [T14, T16].

## 10. Evidence confidence and gaps

- Strong: exercise equals surgery (placebo-controlled surgical trials; long-term RCT in small tears); progressive loaded exercise beats no treatment; supervised volume adds little over a good self-managed program; early motion after repair is safe.
- Moderate: pain rules (trial conventions, untested head to head); scapular and kinetic-chain components; overhead screening limits.
- Weak or missing: minimum effective dose; load intensity; exercise type; post-repair criteria-based progression and return-to-throwing timelines; re-tear by tear size; SPADI/ASES MCIDs from measurement studies; women and adolescents in the trials; the 2024–2026 JOSPT guidance (record seen, text not read).
