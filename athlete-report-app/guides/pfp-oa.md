# Patellofemoral pain and knee/hip osteoarthritis: exercise-based management (BASE Health Noosa guide library)

**Status: draft 1, 2 October 2026, written from published evidence for the clinic to review and edit.** Sources at the end with DOI/PMID (each checked against its Europe PMC record); tags like [T1] point to them. *(consensus)* marks expert agreement; *(practice)* a standard clinical convention without a trial behind it; *(inference)* a working rule that no study tested; *(to verify)* a detail read from a secondary summary that should be checked against the paper before it is relied on.

**Scope and use.** Part A covers patellofemoral pain (PFP) in active adolescents and adults, including runners. Part B covers knee and hip osteoarthritis (OA) in active adults, including the athlete with OA after an earlier knee injury. Patellar tendinopathy is in `tendinopathy.md`; ACL reconstruction in `acl.md`; hip-related groin pain (FAI syndrome) in `groin-pain.md`. The physiotherapist makes the diagnosis, excludes the red lines in section 9 and decides the stage; Claude drafts the program for the condition and stage given, using `rehab-principles.md` for the cross-cutting rehab rules and `training-variables.md` for sets, reps, load and progression. Nothing here replaces a medical review of medication, injections or surgery.

## 1. Rules that matter most

**Patellofemoral pain**

1. **Exercise is the core treatment, and hip plus knee strengthening is the recommended form.** The 2018 international consensus recommends exercise to reduce pain in the short (<6 months), medium (6–12 months) and long term (>12 months), preferring combined hip-and-knee work over knee-only at every time frame *(consensus)* [T1]; the JOSPT guideline grades combined hip- and knee-targeted resistance exercise A [T3]. The living network meta-analysis (22 RCTs, n = 1,472) found no exercise region superior to another [T2], and a three-arm RCT found no difference at 3 months between knee-focused, hip-focused and free physical activity when all three came with education [T21]. The region matters less than doing a progressive program *(inference)*.
2. **Education on load management is a treatment in itself, not a preamble.** In 69 runners, education alone (keep running pain ≤2/10 and settled within 60 minutes; run more often but shorter and slower; avoid hills and stairs; run–walk allowed) did as well at 8 and 20 weeks as education plus strengthening or plus gait retraining: KOS-ADLS 88.8 vs 89.3 vs 83.6 at 8 weeks with no group-by-time effect, and every arm passed the 13.6-point meaningful change by week 4–8 [T16]. In the NMA, education alone had an odds ratio of 9.6 (95% CrI 2.2–48.8) for "any improvement" at 3 months versus wait-and-see, exercise alone 13.0 (2.4–83.5), and education plus exercise plus patellar taping or mobilisation 25.2 (5.7–130.3); by 12 months the arms had largely converged [T2].
3. **Dose is poorly reported, so use a trial-tested template.** Two protocols with published detail: three sessions a week of six hip-and-knee exercises at 3 × 8–12 RM for 8 weeks with three education sessions [T18]; or 3–4 exercises three times a week for ≤20 minutes plus one daily exercise, progressed through four 2-week phases over 8 weeks with five physiotherapy visits [T16]. The consensus names optimal dose as the research priority because exercise prescription is reported so poorly [T1]; the guideline gives no sets, reps or loads [T3].
4. **Use an explicit pain rule.** The guideline sets no threshold [T3]; the only operational rule in a trial is Esculier's running rule: pain ≤2/10 during running and settled within 60 minutes [T16]. For strengthening, apply the acceptable-pain rule from `rehab-principles.md` *(inference)*.
5. **Adolescents need supervised exercise added to education.** In a cluster RCT in adolescent PFP, adding 3 months of exercise during school hours to education raised recovery at 12 months (OR 1.73, NNT 11) and more so at 24 months (OR 2.52, NNT 5); more weekly sessions increased the odds of recovery [T22, pre-2016].
6. **Set expectations: this condition lingers.** Figures quoted in the guideline from primary cohorts: 50–56% of adolescents still have knee pain 2 years after diagnosis; 57% have an unfavourable outcome at 5–8 years; only about a quarter of adolescents and adults report functional recovery at 1 year; recurrence is quoted at 70–90% [T3, to verify against the cohorts]. Build a maintenance plan into every program *(inference)*.
7. **Do not frame hip weakness as the cause.** Across 18 prospective studies (n = 4,818; 483 developed PFP), quadriceps weakness predicted PFP in military recruits (SMD −0.69 normalised to BMI), hip weakness did not (pooled SMDs −0.09 to −0.20), and in adolescents *greater* hip abduction strength was a risk factor (SMD 0.71); age, height, weight, BMI, body fat and Q-angle were not [T20]. Hip strengthening is a treatment that works, not a deficit being corrected *(inference)*.
8. **Adjuncts have narrow roles.** Prefabricated foot orthoses can cut pain short term (no evidence for custom; response varies) [T1], for people with excessive pronation, up to 6 weeks, with exercise (Grade A) [T3]; tailored taping may help for about 4 weeks on top of exercise but not long term (Grade B) [T3]; gait retraining (higher cadence, forefoot strike for rear-foot strikers, less hip adduction) is Grade C [T3], with a 2024 RCT showing running pain at 6 months −3.2 and −2.9 points versus control for impact-reduction and cadence feedback [T17]. Mobilisation alone and electrophysical agents are not recommended [T1, T3].

**Knee and hip osteoarthritis**

9. **Exercise, education and (for knee OA) weight management are the core treatment in every guideline.** OARSI 2019: structured land-based exercise (strengthening, aerobic, balance/neuromuscular), mind–body exercise, weight management with exercise, and education [T4]; NICE 2022: tailored therapeutic exercise (local strengthening plus general aerobic fitness), consider supervision, combine with education [T7]; RACGP 2018: land-based exercise strong for knee and hip, weight loss strong if BMI ≥25 [T8]; EULAR 2023: individualised multicomponent plan, education, tailored exercise dosage and progression, weight management [T5]; ACR 2019: strong recommendations for exercise, weight loss, self-efficacy/self-management programs and tai chi [T23].
10. **The average effect is small to moderate and fades, so plan for maintenance.** Individual-participant-data meta-analysis (31 RCTs, n = 4,241): pain −6.4/100 at 12 weeks, −3.8 at 6 months, −3.4 at 12 months; function +4.5 short term, +3.4 long term [T9]. Aggregate reviews give larger figures: knee OA pain −12/100 and function −10/100 immediately post-treatment, fading to −6 and −3 at 2–6 months [T24, pre-2016]; standardised effect for pain 0.56 at about 8 weeks, peaking at 2 months and no better than usual care after 9 months [T25]. Exercise is still the best-supported non-surgical option; the lesson is to keep people exercising beyond the 6–12-week block *(inference)* [T9, T10, T11].
11. **Exercise type matters less than focus, supervision and frequency.** Aerobic (SMD 0.67), resistance (0.62) and performance/neuromuscular (0.48) programs reduce knee OA pain similarly; single-focus programs beat mixed ones (0.61 vs 0.16); supervised programs three times a week did best, regardless of radiographic severity or baseline pain [T26, pre-2016].
12. **Heavy loading is not needed for pain, but it builds more strength.** In START (377 adults, 18 months, 3 supervised sessions a week), high-intensity (3 × 4–8 at 75–90% 1RM) and low-intensity (3 × 15 at 30–40% 1RM) strength training produced the same WOMAC pain (5.1 vs 4.4 of 20; difference 0.7, 95% CI −0.1 to 1.6) and the same knee compressive force as attention control [T14]. High-intensity programs do add knee flexion strength (SMD 0.39), leg press (0.47) and small KOOS symptom and quality-of-life gains without reducing pain further [T15]. Choose the load by the person's goal and tolerance *(inference)*.
13. **A proven delivery model: 2 weeks of education then 6 weeks of supervised neuromuscular exercise.** GLA:D (registry n = 9,825): two physiotherapist-led education sessions plus one by a former participant, then 12 × 60-minute group sessions, twice a week for 6 weeks; pain (VAS 0–100) fell 12.4 points at 3 months and 13.7 at 12 months, analgesic use and sick leave fell, 5% (knee) and 15% (hip) had a joint replacement within 12 months [T12]. Uncontrolled data, so the size is inflated by regression to the mean [T12].
14. **Exercise is safe for the joint.** Nine RCTs show no harm to cartilage morphology or composition from loading exercise in people at risk of or with knee OA (low-quality evidence) [T27]; START saw no rise in knee compressive force over 18 months, though non-serious adverse events were more frequent with high-intensity training (53 vs 30 vs 4 events) [T14].
15. **Exercise-based care holds up against injections and arthroscopy.** Physical therapy beat intra-articular glucocorticoid injection at 1 year (WOMAC 37.0 vs 55.8; difference 18.8, 95% CI 5.0–32.6) [T28]. For degenerative meniscal tears in middle-aged patients, 12 weeks of exercise therapy equalled arthroscopic partial meniscectomy on KOOS at 2 years (difference 0.9, 95% CI −4.3 to 6.1) with better thigh strength at 3 months; 19% crossed over to surgery without extra benefit [T30]. In people already eligible for knee replacement, surgery plus non-surgical care did better at 12 months than non-surgical care alone (KOOS4 +32.5 vs +16.0) but with more serious adverse events (24 vs 6), and 74% of the non-surgical group had not had surgery at 12 months [T29, pre-2016].
16. **Hip OA evidence is weaker than knee.** Guidelines still list exercise and education as core [T4, T8, T23], but the best exercise type for the hip is undetermined [T8], the pooled hip effect is smaller (−6.7/100, moderate certainty) [T11], and a 12-week physiotherapy program (exercise, manual therapy, education) was no better than sham at 13 weeks for pain (6.9 mm favouring sham, 95% CI −3.9 to 17.7) or function [T31, pre-2016]. Prescribe progressive strengthening plus aerobic work and judge by the individual's response at 6–12 weeks *(inference)*.
17. **OA after knee injury is the athlete's version of this problem.** Odds of knee OA are 4.2 times higher after ACL injury, 6.3 after meniscal injury and 6.4 after combined injury [T32]; a strength and load-management habit after `acl.md` or meniscal rehab is long-term care, not an optional extra *(inference)*.

## 2. Classification and assessment that change the plan

**PFP**

- **Working definition** *(consensus)*: pain around or behind the patella, aggravated by at least one activity that loads the patellofemoral joint on a flexed knee in weight bearing (squatting, stairs, running, hopping or jumping) [T19, wording to verify against the paper]. The 2016 statement covers terminology, clinical examination, natural history, patellofemoral OA and outcome measures [T19]; the 2018 statement covers exercise and physical interventions [T1].
- **Guideline subgroups** (pick the one that dominates and bias the program toward it): overuse/overload without other impairment → load management; muscle-performance deficit → hip and knee resistance training; movement-coordination deficit → gait or movement retraining; mobility impairment → address the restricted structures [T3].
- **Outcome measures graded A**: Anterior Knee Pain Scale (AKPS), KOOS-PF and a pain NPRS/VAS [T3]. Esculier used KOS-ADLS with 13.6 points as the meaningful change [T16].
- **Prognostic factors listed in the guideline**: female sex, single-sport specialisation, longer symptom duration, higher baseline pain, weaker quadriceps and hip abductors/extensors/external rotators, and in severe cases anxiety, depression, catastrophising or kinesiophobia [T3]. In an effect-modifier analysis, weak gluteus minimus (<8.37 N/kg), gluteus medius (<16.82 N/kg) or vastus lateralis (<44.87 N/kg) and a Tampa Scale of Kinesiophobia score >37 favoured adding education to exercise; stronger people did as well with exercise alone (exploratory; males 18–30 only) [T18].
- **Who gets PFP**: quoted prevalence varies 3–85% by population, often cited as about 25%; 43% of military recruits developed it in 6 weeks of intensive training; adolescent female athletes 9.7 per 100 [T3, to verify]; incidence about 10% across prospective cohorts of recruits, adolescents and runners [T20].

**OA**

- **Diagnosis is clinical**; imaging does not set the program. Exercise effects did not depend on radiographic severity [T26] and radiographic severity, age, sex, BMI, comorbidity, muscle strength, symptom duration or baseline physical activity did not moderate the exercise effect in IPD analysis [T9].
- **Who responds more**: higher baseline pain and worse baseline function [T9]; analgesic users (+10.3 KOOS-pain points vs placebo) and people with constant rather than intermittent pain (+10.0) in a GLA:D RCT [T13]; younger people, knee rather than hip OA, and those not awaiting joint replacement in study-level analysis [T25].
- **Baseline measures to record** *(practice, drawn from the programs above)*: KOOS or HOOS (pain, function, quality of life), WOMAC if preferred; 30-second chair stand; 40-m fast-paced walk; bodyweight and BMI; analgesic use; current weekly exercise [T12, T14].
- **Knee versus hip**: knee programs may add dietary weight management; OARSI found no RCT evidence that diet-based weight loss helps hip symptoms [T4]. Patellofemoral OA sits between the two parts of this guide: the ACR conditionally recommends patellofemoral bracing for it [T23].

## 3. Stages and criteria

**PFP (adults and adolescents).** Timings are drawn from the 8-week trial programs and the natural history; they are a template, not a finding *(inference from T16, T18, T22)*.

| Stage | Goal | Content | Progress when | Typical time |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Settle and educate | Understand the condition; cut the aggravating load, not all load; start strengthening | Education on PFP, pacing and load management; running rule ≤2/10 settling in 60 min; shorter, slower, more frequent runs, run–walk, avoid hills/stairs; hip and knee strengthening at tolerable load; prefabricated orthoses or taping if indicated | Daily tasks less painful; exercises tolerated within the pain rule | weeks 0–2 [T1, T3, T16] |
| 2 Build | Progressive hip + knee strength; restore squat, step and lunge depth | 3 sessions/week, 3 × 8–12 RM, 2-week phases of increasing difficulty; running volume progressed within the pain rule | Meaningful PROM change (KOS-ADLS ≥13.6; AKPS or KOOS-PF improving); pain-free step-down and single-leg squat to task depth *(practice)* | weeks 2–8 [T16, T18] |
| 3 Return to full load | Full training and sport-specific loading; address running mechanics if pain persists in runners | Load progression per `training-variables.md`; cadence +7.5–10% or impact-reduction feedback for persistent running pain | Symptoms stable at target volume for 2 weeks; PROM at or near normal | weeks 8–12 [T3, T16, T17] |
| Maintenance | Prevent recurrence (quoted 70–90%) | Keep 2 strength sessions/week; manage load spikes; adolescents keep supervised sessions for 3 months | Review at 3, 6 and 12 months | to 12 months [T3, T22] |

**Knee and hip OA.** Modelled on GLA:D and START with the frequency finding from Juhl *(inference from T12, T14, T26)*.

| Stage | Goal | Content | Progress when | Typical time |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 Education | Understand OA, the role of load and exercise, weight and pain expectations | 2 clinician-led sessions (plus a peer session where available); agree goals and measures | Person can state the plan and the pain rule | 2 weeks [T7, T12] |
| 1 Supervised block | Build the exercise habit; neuromuscular control and strength | 12 × 60-min supervised sessions, 2/week; progressive strengthening of quadriceps, hip extensors/abductors, calf; balance/neuromuscular work; walking or cycling for aerobic fitness | Attendance ≥10 of 12; pain and function improving; chair-stand and 40-m walk improving | 6 weeks [T12] |
| 2 Progression | Single-focus strength (or aerobic) program at 3/week; add load, not variety | Resistance 3 × 8–15 progressed in 9-week cycles; low-load high-rep or heavier load by tolerance; weight management plan if BMI ≥25 | 12-week review of KOOS/HOOS, strength, bodyweight | weeks 7–18 [T14, T26] |
| 3 Maintenance | Hold the gains that otherwise fade by 9–12 months | 2–3 sessions/week of the same focus, supervised check-ins quarterly; re-enter a block after flares or inactivity | Review at 6 and 12 months; refer if deterioration despite adherence | ongoing [T9, T25] |

## 4. Exercise selection by stage

**PFP**

- **Strengthening menu from the trials**: clamshell, side-lying and standing hip abduction, prone and standing hip extension, knee extension, squat and forward lunge, loaded with elastic bands at 3 × 8–12 RM [T18]; standardised lower-limb strengthening and control exercises progressed in difficulty over four 2-week phases [T16]. The guideline accepts both weight-bearing (resisted squats) and non-weight-bearing (resisted knee extension) knee exercise [T3].
- **Order and bias by subgroup**: hip-targeted work may be favoured early [T3]; progress to compound weight-bearing patterns (squat, step-down, lunge) as pain allows *(practice)*; use `training-variables.md` for strength (3–4 × 6–12 at 1–3 RIR) once technique and tolerance are established *(inference)*.
- **If knee extension is too painful to load**: blood-flow-restriction with high-repetition knee exercise may be used, monitoring adverse events (Grade F) [T3]; the consensus lists BFR as uncertain [T1].
- **Runners**: keep running within the pain rule rather than stopping [T16]; gait retraining is a second-line choice for persistent pain: cadence +7.5–10% with a metronome, or feedback to cut tibial acceleration by about half [T16, T17]. The guideline gives no target cadence [T3].
- **Adolescents**: exercise added to education, ideally supervised and scheduled (school hours in the trial), for 3 months [T22].

**OA**

- **Core content**: quadriceps and hip strengthening, general aerobic fitness, balance/neuromuscular control [T4, T7]; START used 6 lower-body plus 4 upper-body/core exercises [T14]; GLA:D uses neuromuscular exercise (NEMEX) in groups of 6–12 [T12]; tai chi and yoga are supported (OARSI core/ACR strong for tai chi) [T4, T23]; aquatic exercise is a conditional option for those who cannot load on land [T4, T8].
- **Pick one focus per block** (quadriceps strength, aerobic capacity or lower-limb performance) rather than a mixed program [T26].
- **Load**: either 3 × 15 at 30–40% 1RM or 3 × 4–8 at 75–90% 1RM gives the same pain result; the heavier option gives more strength and KOOS quality-of-life gain and more minor adverse events [T14, T15]. For an athlete whose goal is strength or return to sport, use the heavier range once tolerance is shown *(inference)*.
- **Hip OA**: strengthening of hip abductors, extensors and flexors plus aerobic work *(practice)*; the trial that failed against sham combined exercise with manual therapy over 12 weeks [T31], so do not expect quick change and review at 6–12 weeks *(inference)*.
- **Walking aids and bracing**: cane (ACR strong), tibiofemoral bracing for tibiofemoral OA (ACR strong), patellofemoral bracing for patellofemoral OA (ACR conditional) [T23]; NICE does not routinely offer insoles, braces or tape unless instability and exercise alone are insufficient [T7].

## 6. Pain and load rules

- **PFP running rule**: pain ≤2/10 during running and settled within 60 minutes; adjust volume, speed and terrain before stopping running [T16]. For strengthening the guideline sets no threshold [T3]; use the acceptable-pain rule in `rehab-principles.md` and keep load inside the 8–12 RM band, raising it when 12 reps are reached [T18] *(inference)*.
- **PFP progression**: 2-week phases of increasing difficulty [T16]; load raised within the RM band [T18]; expect meaningful improvement by week 4–8 [T16] and treatment effects to converge by 12 months [T2].
- **OA discomfort is expected**: NICE advises patients that initial discomfort is normal and that persisting brings long-term benefit [T7]. No trial in this file specifies an OA flare threshold [T12, T14]; use the general rule from `rehab-principles.md` (pain acceptable up to about 5/10 if it settles by the next morning with no lasting rise in swelling or morning stiffness) *(inference)*.
- **OA load**: pain outcome does not depend on load (30–40% vs 75–90% 1RM) [T14], so start with the lighter range for a deconditioned or anxious person and the heavier range for a strength goal; raise one variable per 1–2 weeks in 9-week cycles [T14] *(inference)*.
- **Joint safety**: loading exercise did not damage cartilage across nine RCTs [T27] and did not raise compressive force in START [T14]; minor adverse events were more common with high-intensity work (53 vs 30 vs 4) [T14], so progress heavy loading gradually *(inference)*.
- **Adherence is the main dose problem**: GLA:D reached 83–84% attendance of ≥10 of 12 sessions [T12]; START retained 85% at 18 months [T14]; the PFP trials reached 89% compliance [T16] and defined adherence as ≥16 of 24 sessions [T18]. EULAR names adherence a research priority [T5].

## 7. Return to sport and outcomes

**PFP**

- **No validated return-to-run or return-to-sport criteria exist**; the guideline has no graded return algorithm [T3]. Working criteria *(practice)*: pain within the running rule at target volume for 2 weeks, PROM at or near normal (AKPS, KOOS-PF), pain-free step-down and single-leg squat to task depth, strength symmetric within the clinic's usual limits.
- **Expected course**: meaningful change (≥13.6 KOS-ADLS points) by week 4–8 and held to week 20 in runners treated with education with or without exercise or gait retraining [T16]; at 3 months every active treatment beats wait-and-see for any improvement, but no treatment clearly beats it for worst pain [T2].
- **Long term**: 50–56% of adolescents still have pain at 2 years, 57% an unfavourable outcome at 5–8 years, and only about a quarter report functional recovery at 1 year; at 14–20 years 22% were pain-free and 71% had less pain than at baseline [T3, to verify]. Adolescents who exercised had better recovery at 24 months than at 12 (OR 2.52 vs 1.73) [T22].

**OA**

- **Expected change**: pooled pain −6.4/100 at 12 weeks, −3.4/100 at 12 months (IPD, against non-exercise control) [T9]; knee OA −12.4/100 versus no treatment and hip −6.7/100 in the 2026 overview [T11]; GLA:D registry −12.4 (3 months) and −13.7 (12 months) without a control group [T12]; START WOMAC pain about 5 of 20 at 18 months in all arms [T14]. Between-group MCID thresholds quoted for pain range 8.4–20.0 on 0–100 [T9].
- **Function and performance**: 30-s chair stand +2.3 repetitions and 40-m walk −2.5 s at 3 months in GLA:D [T12]; thigh muscle strength improves within 3 months of exercise therapy [T30]; high-intensity training adds knee flexion and leg-press strength [T15].
- **Durability**: effects peak around 2 months and are no better than usual care after 9 months at study level [T25]; smaller but present at 12 months in IPD [T9]; exercise matches education, manual therapy, analgesics, injections and arthroscopy but not osteotomy or joint replacement at longer follow-up [T11].
- **Return to sport with OA** *(practice)*: no trial criteria; use symptom stability under progressive sport-specific load, strength and hop measures from `acl.md` where the OA follows a knee injury, and the person's goals.

## 8. Adjuncts and what to avoid

- **Recommended with exercise, PFP**: prefabricated foot orthoses short term [T1, T3]; tailored taping for about 4 weeks [T3]; patellar taping or mobilisation inside a combined package [T2, T3]; multi-session gait retraining (Grade C) [T3]; acupuncture may be used for pain though superiority over sham is unknown (Grade C) [T3].
- **Not recommended, PFP**: patellofemoral, knee or lumbar mobilisation alone; electrophysical agents [T1]; dry needling (A against); manual therapy alone (A against); ultrasound, cryotherapy, phonophoresis, iontophoresis, electrical stimulation and laser (B against); EMG or visual biofeedback (B against); knee braces, sleeves or straps (B against) [T3]. Combined physical interventions without exercise are not the aim: exercise stays the core [T1, T3].
- **Recommended, OA**: education and self-management support [T4, T5, T7, T23]; weight management for knee OA [T4, T7, T8]; manual therapy only alongside exercise, short course [T7, T8]; cane and tibiofemoral bracing (ACR strong) [T23]; aquatic exercise, walking aids (conditional) [T4, T8]; topical NSAIDs for knee OA (OARSI level 1A, ACR strong) and oral NSAIDs or intra-articular glucocorticoid for knee OA (ACR strong) are medical decisions, with the caveat that physiotherapy outperformed injection at 1 year [T4, T23, T28].
- **Avoid or do not routinely offer, OA**: acupuncture and dry needling (NICE: do not offer; RACGP conditional against; ACR conditional for, a known disagreement) [T7, T8, T23]; TENS, ultrasound, interferential, laser, pulsed shortwave, neuromuscular stimulation (NICE) [T7]; shockwave, interferential, laser (RACGP) [T8]; routine insoles, braces, tape and splints without instability (NICE) [T7]; lateral-wedge insoles for medial knee OA and patellofemoral braces (RACGP conditional against) [T8]; glucosamine/chondroitin [T7, T8]; routine paracetamol (NICE, OARSI conditional against) [T4, T7]; opioids (OARSI strongly against) [T4]. NICE section numbers come from a summary and should be verified against NG226 [T7].
- **Surgery decisions**: exercise first for degenerative meniscal tears without definite OA [T30]; knee replacement is more effective than non-surgical care alone in those already eligible, at the cost of more serious adverse events, and three-quarters of the non-surgical group avoided surgery for at least a year [T29].

## 9. Red lines

- **PFP, refer or re-examine before programming** *(practice)*: a traumatic onset with effusion, locking or giving way (meniscal, ligament or chondral injury; patellar dislocation); patellar instability; hip or referred pain in an adolescent (slipped capital femoral epiphysis, Perthes disease), apophysitis (Osgood–Schlatter, Sinding-Larsen–Johansson) or a growth-plate complaint; night pain, fever or systemic symptoms; anterior knee pain that is tendon-focused (use `tendinopathy.md`).
- **OA, refer before or during programming** *(practice)*: a hot, swollen joint or sudden severe pain (septic or crystal arthritis); inability to bear weight after a fall; a locked knee; rapid deterioration, night pain, unexplained weight loss or fever; constant pain unresponsive to a well-run 3-month program, which prompts a surgical opinion [T29]. Medication, injections and imaging decisions sit with the GP or specialist [T4, T7].
- **Program red lines** *(inference)*: pain above the agreed threshold that fails to settle within the rule after two adjustments; a new effusion; a sharp fall in function; hip OA that is worse after 6–12 weeks of progressive loading, because the evidence does not show quick gains [T31].
- **Psychological flags**: anxiety, depression, catastrophising and kinesiophobia predict poorer PFP outcomes in severe cases [T3]; a Tampa score >37 favoured adding structured education [T18]. Address them in the plan rather than loading harder *(inference)*.

## 10. Evidence confidence and gaps

- **PFP**: the treatment evidence is low to very low certainty (79% of NMA outcomes at risk of bias) [T2]; the consensus notes that more trials of similar programs may not be warranted and that dose is the open question [T1]. Education alone performs as well as adding exercise or gait retraining in runners [T2, T16, T21], yet exercise remains the recommended core [T1, T3]; the exploratory effect-modifier data suggest the weaker and more fearful gain most from the full package [T18]. No validated return-to-sport criteria. Prognostic figures were read from the guideline rather than the cohorts and are marked *(to verify)*.
- **OA**: guidelines are consistent on the core [T4, T5, T7, T8, T23], though the EULAR update has been criticised for lacking specificity on dose [T6], and the IPD analysis and 2026 overview show small, fading effects and question first-line status [T9, T10, T11]; aggregate reviews report larger effects [T24, T25]. Type and dose were not testable as moderators in the IPD set [T9]; the single-focus and 3-per-week findings come from study-level meta-regression [T26]. No verified OA flare rule, no verified KOOS/HOOS/WOMAC MCIDs, no telehealth RCT, and hip OA RCTs are thin [T31].
- **Pre-2016 items** are flagged in the Sources list (T22, T24, T26, T29, T31) and used only where no newer trial answers the question.
- **Read-depth caveat**: T5 and T11 were read at abstract level; T7's section numbers came from a summarised fetch; the Winters NMA was read from a full-text PDF but its print citation (vol 55:369–377) should be checked.
