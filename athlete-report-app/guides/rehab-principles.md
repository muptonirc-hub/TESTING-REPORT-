# Rehabilitation principles: loading, pain, criteria and return to sport (BASE Health Noosa guide library)

**Status: draft 2, 2 October 2026, written from published evidence for the clinic to review and edit.** Sources at the end with DOI/PMID (every source checked against its Europe PMC record); tags like [T9] point to them. Items marked *(consensus)* are expert agreement, not trial results.

**Scope and use.** The cross-cutting rules Claude applies to any rehabilitation program: how to progress load after an injury, how much pain is acceptable, what strength and symmetry targets mean, how to move between stages, and what the return-to-sport decision rests on. The injury guides (tendinopathy, ACL, muscle strains, ankle, groin, shoulder, knee/hip, low back) add the condition-specific criteria and dosage; the training-variables guide gives the dosing for each adaptation. The physiotherapist owns every diagnosis, stage decision and return-to-sport decision; Claude drafts within the stage the physiotherapist has set.

## 1. Rules that matter most

1. **Progress by criteria, with time as a minimum, never by time alone.** Every guideline and consensus read says so; no trial has tested time-based against criteria-based progression [T11, T20, T21, T29]. Where criteria have been tested against outcomes, they matter: after ACL reconstruction, failing the discharge battery meant 38% reinjury versus 6% for passing, and each month of delay up to 9 months cut reinjury by 51% [T31]; professional athletes who did not meet all six discharge criteria had 4 times the graft-rupture rate [T32].
2. **Load early and progressively; complete rest is not a treatment.** Early mobilisation and loading are grade A for ankle sprain, rest is "not indicated" for Achilles tendinopathy, and early loading is favoured for muscle injury [T25, T27, T29]; starting rehabilitation 2 days rather than 9 days after a muscle injury brought return to sport forward by about 3 weeks in a randomised trial (figures from the trial report; letter format, confirm in the full text) [T37]. Protect for 1–3 days, then load within symptoms [T1].
3. **The response to the last session sets the next dose** *(consensus, 96%)*: symptoms and pain are the main progression criterion; avoid high-strain, high-rate loads early [T20].
4. **Some pain is acceptable in strengthening; sprinting, jumping and return-to-sport tasks are pain-free.** Rehab to a 4/10 limit returned athletes as fast as pain-free rehab (15 vs 17 days) with better strength and fascicle length and no more reinjury [T9]; in chronic musculoskeletal pain, painful exercise gave a small short-term advantage (SMD −0.27) and no medium- or long-term difference [T33], and the 2025 update of 16 trials found no difference either way, so pain need not be avoided [T34]; in Achilles tendinopathy, continuing to run and jump under a pain-monitoring model did no harm [T35]. Experts allow mild pain in general exercise but require pain-free sprinting before return [T20].
5. **A 90% limb symmetry index overstates recovery.** After ACL reconstruction 57% met 90% LSI on quadriceps strength and four hop tests but only 29% met 90% of their own pre-injury capacity; the pre-injury comparison was far more sensitive to second ACL injury (0.82 vs 0.27) [T12]. Quadriceps deficits versus matched controls persist up to 4 years [T13]. A low hamstring:quadriceps ratio (per 10% lower, HR 10.6) predicted graft rupture [T32]. Use symmetry as one input; add absolute values, ratios, rate of force development and fatigued testing [T18].
6. **Avoid load spikes and keep a high chronic base**; the 0.8–1.3 acute:chronic "sweet spot" is a heuristic, and the one cluster-randomised trial of ACWR-guided load management (482 elite youth footballers) prevented nothing (RR 1.01) [T2–T7, T36].
7. **Strength training prevents injury in a dose-dependent way**: RR 0.34 (about two-thirds fewer injuries; each 10% more strength-training volume, >4 percentage points less risk) [T17]; strength training cut injuries to under a third and overuse injuries by half in the earlier review, proprioceptive training by 45%, stretching not at all [T38]. Late-stage rehab should rebuild strength, explosive capacity, movement quality under fatigue and a game-level chronic load [T18, T20, T39, T40].
8. **Low-load blood-flow restriction training matches heavy loading for strength when heavy loading is not possible** (20–50% 1RM, 2–3 sessions/week, 4–12 weeks; low to very low certainty) and beats low-load training without cuffs (g = 0.52) [T14–T16, T41]. Cuff pressures, widths and safety screening follow the 2019 position stand [T42].
9. **NMES and cross-education help early quadriceps recovery after ACL reconstruction**: NMES added to physiotherapy improved strength at 4–12 weeks (SMD 0.73) [T43]; eight weeks of isokinetic training of the *uninjured* knee from week 4 improved reconstructed-limb quadriceps strength at 12 and 24 weeks [T44]. Pooled across musculoskeletal patients, cross-education is not significant, so treat it as an optional extra [T26].
10. **Isometrics are a loading option, not an analgesic shortcut**: across 10 trials isometric exercise was not superior to isotonic exercise for tendinopathy pain; response is variable [T45].
11. **Measure psychological readiness.** Lower ACL-RSI at return to sport went with second ACL injury in athletes ≤20 years (60.8 vs 71.5) [T19]; validated scales are mandatory for clearance in the ISAKOS consensus [T21]; fear of reinjury is the main reason athletes do not return [T11, T22, T30].
12. **Return to sport is a shared decision across a continuum**: return to participation, to sport, to performance; weigh tissue health and load capacity against the expected load and the risk the athlete and team accept (StARRT) [T10, T46].
13. **Supervision matters less than individual prescription and monitoring**: supervised and unsupervised programs did equally well when individualised; education plus loading worked in person or by telehealth; real-time telerehabilitation matched face-to-face care for function and pain [T11, T25, T47].
14. **Maintenance needs intensity, not volume**: strength and muscle size are kept for up to 32 weeks with one session a week and one set per exercise if the relative load is kept; endurance for 15 weeks with two sessions a week or a third of the volume if intensity is kept; older adults need two sessions and 2–3 sets [T48].

## 2. Acute injury: the first days

- **PEACE** (first 1–3 days): protect (unload, pain-guided), elevate, avoid anti-inflammatory modalities, compress, educate. **LOVE** (after): load early within symptoms, optimism, vascularisation (pain-free aerobic work within days), exercise for mobility, strength and proprioception with pain as the guide [T1]. An editorial position, not a trial.
- **Early loading after muscle injury**: rehabilitation started 2 days after injury versus 9 days returned recreational athletes about 3 weeks sooner (randomised, n=50; published as a research letter, so confirm the exact figures in the full text) [T37].
- **Ice and NSAIDs**: PEACE & LOVE argues against both because inflammation is part of repair [T1]; the Aspetar ACL guideline endorses early post-operative cryotherapy for comfort [T11]. Read these as healing vs symptom control: ice sparingly for pain, no routine NSAIDs, medication questions to the doctor.
- **Immobilisation**: at most 10 days and only for severe ankle sprains; otherwise brace and move [T27].
- Early fracture screen for ankle injuries (Ottawa rules: sensitivity 92–100%) [T27]; suspected complete muscle tear (palpable defect, severe difficulty walking, large active knee-extension deficit) goes to a doctor [T29].

## 3. Load management

- Dose by response *(consensus)*: the response to previous loading guides the next dose (96%); the stage of healing matters (91%); individualise to athlete, sport and injury (90%) [T20].
- Acute:chronic workload ratio: the original proposal (acute ≈1 week over chronic ≈4 weeks; 0.8–1.3 "sweet spot", ≥1.5 "danger"; weekly increases <10%) [T2] rests on observational data with mathematical coupling, arbitrary bins, inconsistent dose–response shapes across cohorts [T3–T7], and the only randomised test of ACWR-guided planning (34 teams, 10 months) found no reduction in health problems (difference 1.8 percentage points, RR 1.01) [T36]. Keep the uncontested parts: gradual, individualised progression; no abrupt spikes; a high chronic base protects [T2].
- Rebuild the sport's chronic load deliberately in late rehab: the four pillars of on-field rehabilitation are movement quality, physical conditioning, sport-specific skills and a progressively developed chronic training load, delivered across five field stages from linear running to practice simulation [T39, T40]; use GPS where available *(consensus 83–95%)* [T18, T20].
- Tendons: load as heavy as tolerated at least 3 sessions/week [T25]; the tendon-stiffness dose is in the training-variables guide.

## 4. Pain during rehabilitation

- The muscle-injury RCT: pain-free (0/10) vs pain-threshold (≤4/10) hamstring rehab, same exercises (bridges, hip extensions, eccentric sliders, Nordic variations, 2–3 × 4–12; nine-stage running progression; 2 supervised sessions/week): clearance 15 vs 17 days (NS), isometric strength about 15% better and fascicle length +0.9 cm in the pain-threshold group at 2 months, reinjuries 2 vs 2 [T9]. The 2022 hamstring guideline adopts this [T29].
- Chronic musculoskeletal pain: painful versus pain-free exercise programmes (7 trials, n=385): small short-term pain benefit for painful protocols (SMD −0.27, moderate-quality evidence), no difference in the medium or long term or for function [T33]; the 2024 update (16 trials) found no difference in pain, disability, catastrophising or fear in any term, with very low to low certainty, and concluded pain during exercise may not need to be avoided [T34].
- Tendon pain-monitoring model: continuing running and jumping within a pain-monitoring model during Achilles rehab produced the same improvement as six weeks of active rest (n=38) [T35]; the Achilles guideline rates pain 0–10 during and after loading tasks, progresses by total daily load and irritability, and does not indicate rest [T25].
- Task-specific tolerance *(consensus)*: mild pain acceptable in general exercise (84%); pain-free running before sprinting (86%); pain-free sprinting before return to sport (97%) [T20].
- Working rule for drafts: strengthening to about 3–5/10 that settles within 24 hours; plyometric, sprint and return-to-sport tasks pain-free; any pain that rises session to session means reduce the dose.

## 5. Strength targets and symmetry

- LSI overestimates recovery because the uninjured limb also weakens: 57.1% met 90% LSI vs 28.6% who met 90% of estimated pre-injury capacity on quadriceps strength and four hop tests; sensitivity for a later second ACL tear 0.82 (EPIC) vs 0.27 (LSI) (n=70) [T12]. Quadriceps strength vs matched controls: SMD −1.42 at 0–6 months, −0.92 at 6–18 months, −0.38 at 18–48 months [T13].
- Symmetry still carries information: more symmetrical quadriceps strength before return reduced reinjury [T31]; a lower hamstring:quadriceps ratio at 60°/s raised graft-rupture risk (HR 10.6 per 10%) [T32].
- Thresholds in use: return to running after ACL reconstruction needs quadriceps LSI >80%, eccentric impulse LSI >80% (countermovement jump), full extension, ≥95% flexion, no or trace effusion; discharge for pivoting sports: isokinetic quadriceps and hamstring symmetry 100% at 60°/s, jump and running mechanics >90% [T11]; Delaware–Oslo pass criteria: >90% on quadriceps strength, hop tests, the Knee Outcome Survey and global rating [T31]; Kyritsis' six criteria: isokinetic strength at 60, 180 and 300°/s, running T-test, single, triple and triple-crossover hops [T32]; ISAKOS: LSI 90–100%, movement quality beyond LSI [T21]; hamstring experts did not endorse a fixed 10% deficit rule (66%) and prioritised outer-range eccentric and isometric strength (83%) [T20].
- Add to LSI: absolute values against norms where the clinic has them, rate of force development, and tests under fatigue [T18].
- Dose after ACL reconstruction: very low-certainty evidence; combine open- and closed-chain work; no advantage of high over low intensity after week 8; plyometrics in the advanced phase [T11]. Eccentric hamstring strengthening: 6–12 reps, 2–3 sessions/week, load and range progressed as tolerated (grade B) [T29].

## 6. Stages and criteria-based progression

- Time is necessary but not sufficient; criteria are physical and psychological [T11]. Return to running after ACL reconstruction: no earlier than about 12 weeks (range 8–16) and only with the criteria above [T11].
- Late stage has four pillars: explosive neuromuscular performance, movement quality under fatigue and with attentional-focus cueing, sport-specific retraining with cognitive load, and a progressive chronic training load; test under fatigue; on-field rehab is a mandatory bridge [T11, T18, T39].
- On-field programme (soccer example): five stages — linear movement, multidirectional movement, sport-specific technical skills, sport-specific movements, practice simulation [T40]. The control–chaos continuum frames the same progression from controlled, low-cognitive drills to chaotic, reactive, high-load training [T49].
- Motor learning: external focus of attention, implicit learning, differential learning and self-controlled practice support neuroplastic change after injury and are proposed to reduce second-injury risk [T50].
- Hamstring *(consensus)*: on-pitch running (98%); sprint dosage approaching game intensity (95%); criteria for return: pain-free sprinting (97%), full unrestricted training (93%), athlete readiness (98%), GPS (83%), kinetic-chain assessment (78%); flexibility did not reach consensus (45%) [T20].
- Ankle: no formal test battery; return when sport-specific tasks are safe, weight-bearing and dynamic tasks pain-free, ROM/strength/proprioception restored; Star Excursion Balance Test, timed hop tests and landing mechanics suggested; mild sprains return within 3–10 days [T27].

## 7. Return-to-sport decisions

- Continuum: return to participation → sport → performance. The StARRT framework weighs tissue health and load capacity, the expected loading of the sport, and risk-tolerance modifiers (time of season, athlete's goals, pressure); the decision is shared among athlete, clinicians and coaches with explicit roles; test closed skills (hops, T-drill) and open, reactive skills; use ACL-RSI or I-PRRS [T10, T46]. Context: about 65% return to pre-injury sport and about 50% to competition after ACL reconstruction; Achilles tendinopathy recurs in up to 44% [T10].
- Outcomes of the decision: returning to level I (pivoting) sport carried 4.3 times the reinjury rate of not returning; 51% lower reinjury per month of delay to 9 months; 38% vs 6% reinjury for failing vs passing the battery [T31]; 16.5% graft rupture in 158 professionals, 4 times higher when the six criteria were not met [T32].
- Psychological readiness: ACL-RSI at return 60.9 (second injury) vs 67.2, significant in ≤20-year-olds (60.8 vs 71.5; a cut-off of 76.7 gave 90% sensitivity) [T19]; fear of reinjury and knee confidence are the central items [T30]; screen stress, sleep and perceived recovery; goal-setting, imagery, graded exposure and cognitive-behavioural strategies are supported [T22].
- Guidelines: normalised IKDC, ACL-RSI and Tampa Scale are part of discharge [T11]; validated psychological scales mandatory and no time-only decisions (100%) [T21].
- Reinjury after muscle injury: 2.7× risk after a previous injury, highest in the same season (RR 4.8) and within 8 weeks (OR 13) [T29].

## 8. Adjuncts: what helps and how much

- **Blood-flow restriction**: vs low-load training without cuffs, strength g = 0.52 across 20 clinical studies [T41]; vs heavy-load training in musculoskeletal disorders (7 RCTs, n=303) knee-extensor MVC SMD 0.47 favouring BFR, other outcomes similar; 20–50% 1RM, 2–3 sessions/week, 4–12 weeks; adverse events 0 vs 5 [T14]. Knee OA: pain SMD 0.25 and quadriceps strength 0.46 better than conventional training; 20–30% 1RM, cuff 50–200 mmHg or 70–80% occlusion pressure [T16]. Upper limb: equal to heavy load, better than light load (strength SMD 0.67) [T15]. Methodology and safety: 2019 position stand [T42]. Use early after surgery or when pain limits load, after screening [T11].
- **NMES**: added to standard physiotherapy after ACL surgery, quadriceps strength SMD 0.73 at 4–12 weeks and better function (6 pooled trials) [T43]; recommended very early for activation and swelling [T11].
- **Cross-education**: after ACL reconstruction, 8 weeks of isokinetic quadriceps training of the uninjured knee (60°/s, 3 days/week from week 4) improved reconstructed-limb strength at 12 and 24 weeks (n=48) [T44]; pooled across musculoskeletal patients the effect is not significant, while immobilisation (0.85) and stroke (1.26) models respond [T26]. Low-cost optional adjunct.
- **Isometrics and eccentrics for tendon pain**: all loading types reduce pain; eccentric is not superior; isometric not superior to isotonic (10 RCTs) [T25, T45].
- **Motor learning**: external-focus cueing, implicit and differential learning, dual-task and cognitive load belong in late-stage programs [T18, T50]; neurocognitive training is a named gap [T11].
- **Sleep and stress**: <7 h sleep and psychological distress go with higher injury risk *(consensus)* [T22].

## 9. Prevention programs that double as late-stage content

- Strength training: RR 0.34 across 7,738 participants, dose-dependent, safe [T17]; earlier review: RR 0.32 for strength training, 0.55 for proprioceptive training, overuse injuries RR 0.53, stretching no effect [T38].
- FIFA 11+: 39% fewer injuries (IRR 0.61); the older FIFA 11 had no effect [T23].
- Nordic hamstring programs: hamstring injuries RR 0.49 (0.32–0.74) across 8,459 athletes; RCTs alone 0.52 [T51].
- Copenhagen adduction program: 3 sessions/week for 6–8 weeks pre-season then 1/week in season; groin problems 13.5% vs 21.3% (OR 0.59) [T24].
- Ankle: supervised balance training after a sprain reduces reinjury at 7–12 months (grade A); bracing prevents first-time sprains (grade A) [T27].
- Keep one weekly maintenance dose after return to sport: intensity preserved, volume cut [T24, T48].

## 10. Red lines

- Stop and refer: suspected fracture (Ottawa rules), complete muscle tear, unexplained or worsening swelling, neurological change, calf pain with swelling (thrombosis), fever or night pain, pain and effusion that do not settle before planned surgery [T21, T27, T29]. Spinal red flags follow the IFOMPT framework: a clinical-reasoning pathway rather than a checklist, because most red flags have poor diagnostic accuracy [T52].
- Do not run, sprint, jump or return to sport with pain or effusion [T11, T20].
- Do not use time alone, LSI alone or a single test to clear anyone [T11, T12, T21, T31].
- BFR only after screening; no BFR without the clinic's protocol [T11, T42].
- The physiotherapist makes every diagnosis, stage and return decision; Claude drafts within the stage given.

## 11. Evidence confidence and gaps

- Strong: criteria over time (consensus plus two cohorts linking criteria to reinjury); early loading over rest (guideline grade A; one RCT); strength training prevents injury (high-quality meta-analyses); LSI overestimates recovery; BFR ≈ heavy load for strength (low certainty but consistent); NMES early after ACL surgery.
- Moderate: pain-threshold rehab (one RCT plus consensus; chronic-pain reviews neutral to slightly favourable); psychological readiness and reinjury (cohorts); cross-education after ACL reconstruction (one RCT positive, pooled clinical evidence null).
- Weak: numeric pain ceilings (expert models); absolute strength targets; the minimal maintenance dose (narrative review); number of exercises and adherence.
- Most evidence is from ACL reconstruction and male team-sport athletes; transfer to other injuries, women and recreational patients is by inference.
