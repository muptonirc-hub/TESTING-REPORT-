# Training variables: what the evidence says (BASE Health Noosa guide library)

**Status: draft 2, 2 October 2026, written from published evidence for the clinic to review and edit.** Sources are listed at the end with their DOI/PMID (each checked against its Europe PMC record); tags like [T5] point to them.

**Scope and use.** How to set load, volume, effort, rest, tempo, frequency, exercise choice and progression for the adaptation wanted: maximal strength, power and rate of force development, hypertrophy, local muscular endurance, speed and change of direction. Healthy trained and untrained adults and athletes, with short notes on youth, older adults and women. The clinic's `programming_guide.md` says how BASE programs from a report; this file is the evidence behind the training variables and fills the gaps. Where the two disagree, the clinic guide wins and the disagreement should be flagged for the physiotherapists. Rehabilitation-specific loading (pain rules, tendon, post-injury) is in the rehab guides.

---

## 1. Rules that matter most

1. **Heavy loads build 1RM strength; growth is load-independent.** Strength gains favour loads ≥80% 1RM (SMD about 0.6 vs light loads), while muscle growth is similar from ~30% 1RM upward when sets are taken close to failure [T1–T4, T67]. In the largest network meta-analysis (178 strength and 119 hypertrophy trials) higher-load, multi-set, thrice-weekly training ranked first for strength and higher-load, multi-set, twice-weekly training first for hypertrophy, with all prescriptions beating control [T76].
2. **Strength is specific.** Gains are largest in the lift, range of motion, contraction mode and limb configuration trained: free-weight vs machine, unilateral vs bilateral, eccentric vs concentric, partial vs full range [T17, T25, T28, T29]. Test what you train.
3. **Volume drives growth and strength with diminishing returns**, steeper for strength. Each extra weekly set added about 0.37% to muscle growth in the 2017 meta-regression [T68]; count direct sets fully and indirect (synergist) sets as half [T4, T5]. For trained adults 12–20 hard sets per muscle per week is a defensible default [T6]; one hard set of 6–12 reps at 70–85% 1RM, 1–3 times a week, still adds about 12 kg to 1RM in trained men [T7].
4. **Training to failure adds nothing to strength and only a small, uncertain edge for growth**; it costs fatigue and power. Stop 1–3 reps short (10–20% velocity loss) for strength, speed and jump outcomes; go closer to failure (up to 40% velocity loss) only when growth is the goal [T8–T11].
5. **Rest: 60–90 s is enough for growth; trained lifters need more than 2 min for strength; 2–5 min for strength-power work** [T13, T14, T32]. Agonist–antagonist supersets cut session time ~37% with no loss of adaptation [T30].
6. **Tempo is a weak lever (0.5–8 s per rep gives the same growth); intent is a strong one.** Fast concentric intent, even against a heavy or immovable load, improves rate of force development [T16, T21]; ballistic-intent isometrics improve rapid force production more than ramped ones [T75]. Accentuated eccentric loading gives no chronic advantage [T18]; flywheel gives a small power edge only [T19, T20].
7. **Frequency works mainly by adding volume.** Two or more sessions per muscle per week is reasonable; with weekly sets equal, frequency changes little [T5, T23, T24].
8. **Power needs a strength base plus its own loads**: the heaviest loads for strength, about 30% 1RM for jump, 40–70% 1RM for power [T4, T32]. Plyometric programmes work best over more than 7 weeks and 14 sessions at 3 sessions a week [T33, T35].
9. **Periodisation helps strength (ES 0.3–0.4) but not growth; undulating beats linear only in trained people** [T49, T50]. Progress roughly 0.03 effect-size units a week; gains are fastest early [T4, T21].
10. **A full week off costs strength, not size.** Reduce rather than stop; tapers cut volume 30–50% and keep intensity ≥85% 1RM [T51–T53]; cessation lowers every strength component (submaximal strength SMD −0.62), more in older and inactive people [T77].
11. **Concurrent endurance training blunts explosive outcomes only** (SMD −0.28), more so in the same session; separate by 3 h or lift first [T56, T57].
12. **Tendons adapt to high strain, not light work**: about 90% MVC, 3 s contractions, 5 × 4, 2 min rest; loading magnitude, not contraction type, drives stiffness gains, and programmes ≥12 weeks tend to do more [T58, T59, T78].

## 2. Load (intensity)

- Bands used in the evidence: low <60% 1RM (>15RM), moderate 60–79% (9–15RM), high ≥80% (≤8RM) [T3].
- **Hypertrophy**: high vs low load effect sizes 0.06 (whole muscle) and 0.05 (lean mass), not significant [T2]; network meta-analysis: no pair of load bands differed (SMDs −0.09 to 0.20) [T3]. Studies showing poorer growth with light loads were work-matched with sets stopped far from fatigue [T1]. Moderate loads (roughly 6–15 reps) are the most time-efficient default [T1].
- **Maximal strength**: high load ES 0.34 (1RM) and 0.41 (isometric) over low load [T2]; NMA high vs low SMD 0.60–0.63, moderate vs low 0.34, high 98% probability of being best [T3]; across 295 studies the heaviest loads were optimal for strength [T4]. The heavy-load advantage shrinks when strength is tested on a different lift, and light loads (≥20 reps) still raise 1RM [T1].
- **Local endurance**: weak evidence for light loads, mainly lower body [T1]; continuous sets beat cluster sets (SMD −0.38) [T15].
- **Power and jump**: ~30% 1RM for vertical jump, 40–70% 1RM for power [T4]; see section 9.
- Untrained people grow more from any load; trained people benefit from more sessions [T3].

## 3. Volume, minimum dose and maintenance

- Both growth and strength rise with weekly sets (100% posterior probability) with diminishing returns, steeper for strength; a fractional count (direct set = 1, indirect = 0.5) fits best [T5]. Pooled dose modelling: +0.05 ES per set and +0.03 ES per week up to 26 weeks; volume, frequency, training status and sex added little once load and sets were known [T4].
- The 2017 meta-regression (15 studies, 34 groups): +0.023 ES (+0.37% growth) per weekly set; higher vs lower volume within studies +3.9%; 10+ sets per muscle per week trended best [T68]. Trained men: optimum about 12–20 weekly sets per muscle; >20 sets added only for triceps in one review [T6]. A numeric ceiling is not established [T5].
- **Minimum effective dose (trained men)**: 1 set × 6–12 reps @ 70–85% 1RM to volitional failure, 1–3 sessions/week, 8–12 weeks: 1RM +12.1 kg (squat +17.5, bench +8.3) [T7].
- Drop sets, rest-pause and cluster sets match traditional sets when volume and effort are matched (hypertrophy g = 0.05) [T31].
- **Layoffs**: one week of complete rest mid-programme left size unchanged but blunted strength gains (squat +13.0 vs +16.4 kg) [T51]; after a programme ends, strength stays above baseline for 16–24 weeks and is back to baseline by about 48 weeks; size is lost faster [T54]. A soccer off-season without training cost VO2max (ES −1.48) and jump (−0.81); a maintenance programme roughly halved the loss [T55].

## 4. Effort: proximity to failure, velocity loss, RIR accuracy

- **Strength**: no relationship with proximity to failure [T8]; failure vs non-failure ES −0.09; when volume is not equated non-failure wins (ES −0.32) [T10]; velocity-loss threshold does not change strength [T11].
- **Hypertrophy**: growth rises as sets approach failure [T8], but pooled failure vs non-failure effects are small and borderline (0.12–0.22, CIs crossing zero), with a small edge in trained people [T9, T10]; +0.006 ES per % velocity loss [T11].
- **Power**: higher velocity loss worsens jump, sprint and bar speed; use 10–20% velocity loss (about 1–3 RIR) when speed matters, up to 40% only for growth [T11].
- **RIR accuracy**: people underestimate reps left by ~1; accuracy is good near failure, with heavier loads (≤12 reps) and on later sets; poor far from failure [T12]. Prescribe 0–3 RIR with confidence; treat "5 RIR" as a rough guide.

## 5. Rest between sets

- Hypertrophy: >60 s gives a small benefit, nothing added beyond ~90 s (short vs long comparisons trivial, SMD 0.13–0.17) [T13].
- Strength: trained lifters >2 min; untrained 60–120 s is enough; some gain even with <60 s [T14]. Strength-power sessions 2–5 min [T32].
- Power and velocity: cluster sets or intra-set pauses keep bar speed (SMD 0.18), shift the force–velocity profile toward velocity (0.28) without changing strength or size [T15].
- Endurance: shorter rests and continuous sets [T14, T15].
- Contrast pairs: intra-pair rest 0–8 min, mode 3 min; individualise to strength level [T37, T38].
- Supersets: −37% session time, volume load, reps, strength and size unchanged; agonist–antagonist pairs increase reps (SMD 0.68); pairing similar movements reduces volume load (−1.08) [T30].

## 6. Tempo, eccentric emphasis, isometrics and intent

- Repetition durations 0.5–8 s produce similar growth; no tempo wins for strength; very slow tempos (>10 s) lack evidence and force lighter loads. Pattern: controlled eccentric, fast concentric. Time under tension per set about 2–20 s for strength, 20–70 s for growth [T16].
- Eccentric-only training raises eccentric strength (27% vs 10%, g = 1.51) but not concentric or isometric strength; fast eccentrics transfer poorly to slow tests [T17]. Accentuated eccentric loading (e.g. 60% up, 80–120% down): no chronic advantage for 1RM, jump or size; far higher RPE (SMD 1.72) [T18].
- Flywheel vs traditional: power SMD 0.47, strength not different; advantage only over weight-stack machines; 6 weeks, 2–3 sessions/week, ≥48 h apart; overload is not guaranteed and should be confirmed with an encoder [T19, T20].
- **Rate of force development**: resistance training improves RFD at 50 ms (SMD 0.58) and 200 ms (0.39); high-speed training 0.54, slow training with fast intent 0.41, slow without intent 0.21 [T21]. Early RFD (<75 ms) is neural; maximal strength explains 18% of RFD at 10 ms and 78% at 200 ms [T22]. Cue maximal intent on every strength rep.
- **Isometric training** (two reviews): for maximal strength 80–100% MVC, 1–5 s holds, 30–90 s total contraction per session, at several joint angles or the target angle; for hypertrophy 70–75% MVC, 3–30 s holds, >80–150 s per session over >36 sessions; ballistic intent maximises RFD [T74]. Long-muscle-length isometrics grow muscle more (0.86–1.69%/week vs 0.08–0.83%) and transfer better to dynamic performance; ballistic intent gives more neural activation and rapid force; ≥70% intensity is needed for tendon change [T75]. Isometrics cause less fatigue and give joint-angle-specific strength, useful when mobility is limited by injury [T74].

## 7. Frequency

- Growth, volume equated: 2×/week beat 1×/week in a 2016 review (ES 0.49 vs 0.30) [T24]; the 2025/26 meta-regression finds the frequency effect compatible with zero once volume is modelled [T5].
- Strength: volume equated, no effect; not equated, graded gains 0.74/0.82/0.93/1.08 for 1/2/3/4+ sessions, with the effect in multi-joint lifts, the upper body, younger adults and women [T23]; the meta-regression finds strength rising with frequency with diminishing returns [T5].
- Plyometrics: 3 sessions/week beat fewer for reactive strength [T33]; weekly frequency moderated plyometric hypertrophy [T34].
- Practical: spread sets over ≥2 sessions; add frequency to add volume or heavy practice.

## 8. Exercise selection and order

- **Free weights vs machines** (13 RCTs, 1,016): strength is modality-specific; no difference in size, isometric strength or jump [T28].
- **Unilateral vs bilateral**: same growth; bilateral tests favour bilateral training (0.56), unilateral tests favour unilateral (−0.65) [T29].
- **Range of motion**: full ROM has a trivial overall edge (SMD 0.12); strength is gained in the range trained; long-length partials may favour growth (SMD −0.28, wide CI) and beat short-length partials (ES 0.28) [T25, T26], but the best RCT found lengthened partials equal to full ROM in trained adults [T27]. Default to full ROM for function; use long-length partials when range is limited.
- **Multi-joint first**: the frequency–strength effect existed only for multi-joint lifts [T23]; heavy multi-joint lifts transferred best to change of direction [T48]; weightlifting derivatives beat traditional lifts for jump (g = 0.95) but not strength [T39].
- **Order** (11 studies): strength gains are largest in the exercises done first; multi-joint-first favours multi-joint strength (ES 0.32) and single-joint-first favours single-joint strength (ES −0.58); order does not change hypertrophy [T69]. Put the priority exercise first; power and the main lift early [T37].
- Advanced systems (drop sets, rest-pause, cluster, tempo) equal traditional sets when volume and effort match; rest-pause has a small growth edge [T31].

## 9. Power, RFD, plyometrics, contrast, velocity-based training

- Loads: heaviest for strength, ~30% 1RM for jump, 40–70% for power; combine heavy and light in a programme; weaker athletes build strength first; 2–5 min rest [T4, T32]. Strength underpins jumping, sprinting and change of direction, potentiation and injury risk; relative strength is staged as deficit, association and reserve, and the stage sets the training emphasis [T71].
- **Plyometrics**: reactive strength index ES 0.54 across 61 studies; adults > youth; >7 weeks, >14 sessions and 3 sessions/week superior; total jumps ≤1,080 vs >1,080 did not differ [T33]. Plyometrics also grow muscle (SMD 0.47) [T34]. In young footballers jump and sprint ES 0.60–0.98 [T35]. Umbrella: moderate–large jump, small sprint, moderate–large COD effects [T36].
- **Weightlifting derivatives** vs traditional: jump g = 0.95, strength not different [T39].
- **Complex/contrast** (27 team-sport studies): complex (heavy set alternated with a light ballistic set) and contrast (all heavy sets, then all light) both improve 1RM (SMD 2.01 / 1.29), jump (0.88 / 0.55), sprint (−0.94 / −0.27) and COD (−1.17 / −0.68), complex slightly more [T70]; typical design 4–16 weeks, 2 sessions/week, 3 sets, heavy lift ≥85% 1RM paired with body-weight ballistic work, intra-pair rest mode 3 min [T38]; traditional training is best for 1RM when power is not the aim [T37].
- **Velocity-based training** vs %-based (17 studies, trained): jump SMD 0.27, COD 0.45, 1RM 0.21 (NS) [T42]. Autoregulation vs fixed loading in athletes ES 0.64 (APRE 0.78) [T43]. Velocity loss 10–20% for speed outcomes [T11].
- **Force–velocity profiling**: one 2026 meta-analysis favours individualised training for jump (g = 0.82) [T40]; a 2025 meta-analysis calls the benefit unclear (6.5%, 90% CI 1–12%) and raises blinding concerns [T41]. Treat as optional.

## 10. Speed and change of direction

- Elite sprint practice: sprint at ≥95% or <70% of maximal velocity, 2–3 maximal sessions/week with ≥48 h between; strength and plyometrics address limiting factors but do not replace sprinting [T44].
- **Resisted sprinting**: improves 0–10 m (ES 0.55) with no change in maximal velocity [T45]; in athletes sprint SMD 0.65 vs 0.42 unresisted, COD 1.10 vs 0.60, typical sled load 10–20% body mass, 4–12 weeks, 2–4 sessions/week [T46]. Assisted sprinting raises step frequency [T45].
- Female team-sport athletes: reactive (plyometric) and combined methods beat maximal-strength-only methods for sprint; >8 weeks and >12 sessions [T47].
- **COD**: plyometrics improved COD 5.3% vs 1.0% in controls (47 of 49 groups); heavy multi-joint strength −1.7% to +12.7%; no method is clearly best and transfer depends on angle and distance [T48]. VBT beat %-based for COD (0.45) [T42].

## 11. Periodisation, progression, deloads, tapers, concurrent training

- Periodised > non-periodised for 1RM (ES 0.43); volume-equated 0.31; undulating > linear (0.31) in trained people only; no growth difference [T49, T50].
- Progression: +0.03 ES/week to 26 weeks; +0.05 ES per set [T4]; RFD and strength gains largest early [T21]. Progress one variable at a time.
- **Deloads**: competitive lifters deload every ~5.6 weeks for ~6 days, cutting sets, load and effort but keeping frequency [T52]; the one RCT of a full week off found no benefit and a strength cost [T51]. Reduce, don't stop.
- **Taper**: 1–2 weeks, volume −30–50%, intensity ≥85% 1RM, step or exponential; expect +1–6% in squat/bench; >7 days fully off risks detraining [T53].
- **Detraining**: cessation reduces submaximal strength (SMD −0.62), maximal force and power, more in older (>65 y) and inactive people and with longer breaks (103 studies) [T77]; strength stays above baseline for 16–24 weeks after a programme ends [T54].
- **Concurrent training**: strength −0.06 and size −0.01 (no interference); explosive strength −0.28, worse in the same session (−0.31); separate by ≥3 h or lift first [T56, T57].

## 12. Tendon loading, special groups, warm-up

- **Tendon**: loading raises stiffness (SMD 0.74) via material change, not size; ~5% strain protocols work (stiffness 1.04), ~3% do not; intensity ≥70% better; contraction mode does not matter; adults ≥60 y adapt less [T58]. The 2015 meta-analysis (27 studies) agrees: stiffness SMD 0.70, modulus 0.69, CSA 0.24; loading magnitude matters, contraction type does not; ≥12-week programmes tend to do more [T78]. Prescription: ~90% MVC, 3 s per repetition, 5 × 4, 2 min rest; low-intensity work does not reach the needed strain [T59].
- **Youth**: resistance training improves strength (SMD 0.5–1.1), power, sprint and COD; free weights > machines for strength; pre-PHV low-load high-velocity technique work, post-PHV heavy strength 2–4 × ≤6 @ 80–89% 1RM; ≥2 sessions/week; safe when supervised [T60, T61]; the 2014 international consensus endorses supervised, technique-led youth resistance training [T72].
- **Older adults**: power training at 20–70% 1RM with maximal concentric intent is safe and effective within 12 weeks [T62]; heavy-load 1RM advantage is smaller and tendon adaptation attenuated [T2, T58]; the NSCA position statement covers program design, adaptations and frailty/sarcopenia considerations [T73].
- **Women**: relative hypertrophy equal to men (absolute slightly less, SMD 0.19) [T63]; frequency–strength effect significant in women [T23].
- **Warm-up/PAPE**: after a thorough, task-specific warm-up the extra effect of a conditioning activity is small (ES 0.01 vs 0.16 after a minimal warm-up); 6–10 min between conditioning set and performance; plyometric conditioning > isometric > traditional [T64, T65]. 7–10 min dynamic stretching improves jump (+1.6 cm) and sprint; static stretching alone does not [T66].

## 14. Evidence confidence and gaps

- Strong, consistent: load–strength specificity; load-independence of growth; failure not needed for strength; frequency acting through volume; rest ≥60–90 s for growth and >2 min for trained strength; periodisation for strength; concurrent training harming only explosive outcomes [T1–T5, T8–T14, T23, T24, T49, T50, T56].
- Moderate: volume dose–response shape and any ceiling (numeric plateau not verified) [T5, T6]; velocity-loss thresholds [T11]; plyometric dose [T33]; tendon strain thresholds [T58, T59].
- Uncertain or conflicting: lengthened partials vs full ROM [T25–T27]; force–velocity individualisation [T40, T41]; deload design (one RCT, full rest only) [T51, T52]; isometric training dose for strength (no meta-analysis verified); detraining time course and the minimal maintenance dose (Spiering 2021 not verified); resisted-sprint load bands [T45, T46].
- Population caveat: most trials are young men (79% male in [T5], 89% in [T11]); athlete, female and older-adult data are thinner.
- Uncertain or conflicting (continued): isometric training dose for strength now rests on two reviews, not a meta-analysis [T74, T75]; the minimal maintenance dose is in `rehab-principles.md` (Spiering 2021).
- Not verified in this pass (for the clinic to check): Weakley 2021 VBT review (journal not indexed); Radaelli 2024 older-adult volume network meta-analysis; the 2026 Sports Med individual-variables meta-analysis.
